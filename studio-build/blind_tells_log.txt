WHAT GIVES OUR QUESTIONS AWAY (from blind tests: in round 1 an expert picked out 30 of 34 of our completions and 0 of 61
real ones). Fix ALL of these - rewriting content where needed, not just polishing words. Keep each item's mechanism, key,
answer position and difficulty; re-solve after every change (exactly one defensible answer).

1. REGISTER. Real NITE English is a professional translation from Hebrew: formal, slightly stiff, literal, plain.
   Ours was smooth native/idiomatic magazine English. Remove idioms and colloquialisms ("would do well", "picking apart",
   "trial run", "at a dizzying rate", "of modest means", "as a matter of course", "passed over", "That said", "Quite the
   opposite", "hurts no one", "may well"). Prefer literal, somewhat formal phrasing: "is of the opinion that", "has no
   choice but to", "one of the reasons why", "it is not surprising that", "in contrast to". (Do not insert grammar errors.)
2. TOPICS. Real items favor academic / historical / cultural / scientific / abstract-ethical matter, often dated or
   invented (a historian's view of a king, an invented culture or tribe, a researcher's theory, a philosopher, a
   poet's work, an old custom, a company with a plain invented name, an invented vitamin). Ours used present-day
   consumer/tech/civic life (navigation apps, gyms, solar panels, cafés, marathons, ticket prices, street festivals).
   Move scenarios toward the real mix; everyday scenes are fine occasionally, but plain and old-fashioned, never trendy.
3. NAMES. Real: title + surname (Professor Hochberg, Dr. Mizrahi) or surname alone, or plain/classic first names
   (David, Joseph, Shlomo, Yoram, Miriam, Ruth). Ours: trendy Israeli first names (Rotem, Lavi, Itai, Omer, Noa, Ido)
   and modern full names (Maya Katz, Dana Ronen). Use fewer names overall; a researcher is "Professor X" or "the
   historian X".
4. PRONOUNS. Real NITE uses generic "he/his" (not singular "they"). Use "he" for generic persons and a named person's
   own gender. (This overrides the earlier "generic they" rule.)
5. ITEM SHAPE. Real completions are one or two dense sentences with 3-4 blanks, where the trap is in the logic
   connectors. Ours were sometimes LSAT/argument puzzles dressed as completions (analogy mapping, principle
   application, "Dr. A: ... Dr. B: I disagree. ____" dialogues, litotes tricks, counterfactuals on rhetorical questions,
   modern study write-ups). Recast into the real shape.
6. OPTIONS. Ours repeated a long padded phrase verbatim in 3-4 options, used near-synonym distractor pairs
   (turned down/declined), tidy mirrored symmetry (4x/2x vs 1/2, 1/4), and asides that seal the logic ("which are
   known to hurt school performance", "After all, it is well known that ..."). Real options are shorter, messier,
   unequal, and distractors sometimes wander slightly. No explanatory asides.
7. OPENINGS/PUNCTUATION. No magazine openers ("What makes a novel worthy ...?", "Does relying on ...?"), no colon +
   list of three with Oxford comma, no "Even so," he added. Open plainly: "Professor X claims that ...", "Although ...",
   "It is not surprising that ...".
8. DIFFICULTY. Items that are one-dimensional with no trap look fake; every item needs the real kind of trap.

ROUND 1, PARAGRAPH ITEMS: 47 of 47 caught. Additional tells:
9. REAL CONTENT. Real short texts are excerpts about REAL, checkable people, works, places and facts (Jane Jacobs on Le
   Corbusier, Hubble's Cepheid, de Waal's bonobos, Kovalevskaya, Bilu), often with Israeli context (shekels, Israeli
   universities, Hebrew names, Israeli history). Ours used invented experts (Helen Marsh, Lena Farrow, Ruth Amsel),
   invented places (Merrow Bay, Kalden, Morvania) and invented words. NEW RULE: build short texts and scenarios on REAL,
   ACCURATE, well-documented material wherever the mechanism allows: a real scholar's actually held view, a real
   historical episode, a real scientific finding, a real custom - described correctly (the old rule stands only in
   this form: never ATTACH AN INVENTED finding/quote/opinion to a real person or institution). Use only facts you are
   confident about and that are widely documented; paraphrase, do not invent quotes. Invented material only where
   the mechanism truly needs it (logic puzzles, everyday dialogues with plain Hebrew names).
10. NO CLONES OF THE SOURCE. The mirrored real item must not be recognizable: keep the question TYPE and the KIND of
   trap, but do not reproduce its skeleton (same paragraph moves, same option layout, same stem formula + same
   distractor order). An expert who knows the real item should not be able to pair ours with it.
11. SOURCE LINES. Real short texts usually carry a plain source line ("The following appeared in an article about ...",
   "The following is an excerpt from a book by historian X ...") or none; avoid generic invented ones and avoid texts
   that are obviously written-to-order puzzles (date arithmetic, rate/probability comparisons, unit mix-ups,
   textbook statistics examples, "combine two psychology effects").
12. LENGTH/DENSITY. Match the real distribution: no tiny two-sentence texts and no long semicolon chains; options
   uneven and concrete, full of the text's own detail, not tidily balanced. Keys should not hinge on one planted hedge.
13. Language examples: the real exam is translated from Hebrew, so items about English word formation stand out; if a
   language example is needed, make it general (or about a language described in the text), not English-specific.

ROUND 1, COMPLETIONS PART 2 - extra tells:
14. KEY TELEGRAPHED. Our correct option was often the longest / most detailed / most hedged; or three near-synonym
   distractors vs one unique key ("in recent years / in our own time / in the present day" vs "a century ago"); or a
   mechanical grid of a phrase and its negation in every combination. Real option sets are uneven in a way that does
   NOT point to the key, and distractors differ in meaning, sometimes oddly.
15. One-blank items: real ones offer same-part-of-speech words that differ in MEANING; ours tested grammatical form
   ("recognize right away / instantly recognizable") - never do that.
16. No encyclopedic popular-science prose with one sentence-length blank; no "set up a rule then apply it" completions.
17. Anglo-American settings (Oak Street bakery, quarterly reports, community theater, city council, assistant coach)
   stand out; real ones are Israeli, historical, academic or plainly invented (Zviel, Gormezia, vitamin Q).

ROUND 1, PARABLES / STRENGTHEN-WEAKEN - extra tells:
18. INVENTED SAYINGS. Polished pastoral/nautical proverbs that fit the scenario exactly ("No one mops the deck while
   the waves are still breaking over it") are the strongest tell. Real items use everyday comparisons (oatmeal, a
   duck and a swan, uphills and downhills, a bridge over a river) or real, known sayings.
19. NAMING EVERYTHING. Real items name things sparingly. Do not give quoted brand names, album/band titles, street or
   climbing-wall names, or Hebrew-looking invented disease names in Nordic-sounding countries. At most one plain
   invented country name (like Lundia) when needed.
20. Dashes: plain hyphen " - " only; never the en dash "–".
21. TEXTBOOK CONFOUNDS. One clean named-fallacy key (self-selection, base rate, survivorship, reporting rate) in a
   GMAT-style setup reads as imitation; real strengthen/weaken items are rooted in a concrete, somewhat messy
   situation, and distractors are odder and less tidy. Avoid famous psychology-textbook effects as scenarios.
22. Use only stem formulas attested in the real sample (see your profile); avoid "could be a consideration against",
   "could lead X to reconsider", "In X's case, what corresponds to", "casts doubt on the reliability of".
23. Perfectly parallel option templates ("A woman who knows that... / A man who knows that...") are a tell; vary
   options as the real ones do.

24. SELF-CONTAINED. Real content is welcome, but the key must be decidable from the text/sentence itself, never from
   outside knowledge (which philosopher held which view, what the Black Death did to wages). State what is needed.

ROUND 2 LESSONS (retests: completions pt1 20/34 caught, parables/weaken 48/54 caught) - these REFINE earlier points:
25. RULE 9 IS NARROWED: real famous people and encyclopedia facts belong in PARAGRAPH (topic 44) and READING items,
   where real NITE quotes articles/books. In COMPLETIONS, PARABLES/COMPARISONS, STRENGTHEN/WEAKEN, SCIENCE and
   LOGIC items, famous people / textbook cases (Darwin, Mendel, Kafka, Galileo, Aesop, Odysseus, Newton, Matthew
   effect, Simpson's paradox, Berkeley admissions, endowment effect) are now the #1 tell. There, use SPECIFIC but
   INVENTED people/places with slightly odd names (Professor Igleson, Zviel, Mr. Mekam, the Dolmanian sculptor
   Ptolemy, Beznia, Upper Barbonia, Lundia and Sarvia, the Hothouse Theater, "vitamin Q", the Galilee Egg Company)
   and odd concrete scenes (a compote, a king's chamomile, an orchestra's driver). Not generic actors ("A farmer",
   "A company", "In a certain village") and not trendy first names.
26. SAYINGS: stock English idioms (cart before the horse, gift horse, golden goose, stable door, teach a man to fish)
   are as much a tell as polished invented proverbs. Real items use sayings that sound translated/foreign and a
   little odd ("Every duck is a swan in its lover's eyes"), or concrete odd comparisons.
27. OPTIONS - run python3 studio-build/lint_originals.py <GROUP> and make it pass: the key must not be the longest
   option in more than 30% of items; distractors as detailed, specific and plausible as the key (never one-line
   filler, never irrelevant trivia); no "since"/semicolon explanation only in the key; no qualifier in the key's
   wording that gives it away; long options are often WRONG in real items.
28. DISTRACTORS fail by subtle logic (stacked negation, counterfactual, a strengthener hidden among weakeners, a
   reversed condition), not by crude antonym flips ("rose/fell", "accepted/rejected") or plain contradictions.
29. STOCK TEMPLATES to avoid: "X hypothesized... To his surprise...", the harsh critic whose "not bad" is praise, the
   new teaching method veteran teachers resist, one clean textbook flaw (base rate, reverse causation, selection,
   better diagnosis) with an obvious answer. Real weaken items often need two steps.
30. EVERY ITEM MUST HAVE EXACTLY ONE DEFENSIBLE ANSWER - round 2 found items with two keys or none (vo-41-101,
   vo-41-119, vo-41-103). Re-solve every item cold after editing.
31. NAMES, CORRECTED (replaces point 3's list): the anglicised Bible set (Joseph, David, Ruth, Miriam, Shlomo,
   Esther) and stock surnames (Cohen, Levi, Mizrahi, Rosen, Katz, Shapira, Harel, Adler) are now a tell. Real items
   use modern Israeli transliterations (Nechama, Chagit, Bentzi, Na'or, Gilad, Oded, Avner, Aya, Bella, Galit, Dorit,
   Galia), often in Hebrew alphabetical order for sets of people (Aya, Bella, Galit / Amir, Bentzi, Galia, Dorit),

32. Real items do not lecture on a term (loanword, minimal pair, sentence types, symmetric relations) - avoid
   textbook-term setups. Stems must not explain their own logic; options must not carry their own justification
   ("since not every tree..."). Real quotes are obscure and long, clumsily translated - not famous Western maxims.
33. Translation scars in real items: "if X - Y" conditionals, stems ending "... -", "According to what X", comma-
   splice "however". Use these patterns naturally where they fit (do not insert spelling errors).
34. DISTRACTORS MUST BE LOCALLY SENSIBLE. Each wrong completion option must read as coherent on its own and survive
   the first blank; it fails only when the whole sentence's logic is followed. Never a self-contradicting option,
   never an off-topic word at blank 1. Often two options differ in one slot only.
35. No graded-intensifier option sets ("less / considerably / as expected / much more") and no hedged key
   ("may ... may ...").
36. RULES/ARRANGEMENT items (topic 48): real ones are small and everyday (three people's salaries, four cars, a
   square table, a school's bylaws), wordy but logically FLAT. Avoid legal drafting ("- and only on such days -",
   "Notwithstanding the above", defined terms in quotes, labelled taxonomies), abstract symmetric tables (salts
   P/Q/R/S, caterpillars A/B/C), knights-and-knaves lying, puzzles on English spelling/letters, exhaustive 2x2
   option layouts, recent specific years and shekel tiebreaks, and loophole-closing clauses whose only job is to
   block alternative readings.
37. SCIENCE items (topic 47): no famous textbook studies (Simpson/Berkeley, chess memory, endowment, Hawthorne,
   thixotropy) and no taught named designs (lottery randomization, knockout mouse). Real ones use quirky invented
   settings with Israeli names and odd details.
38. READING (and any paragraph reference): real NITE points to places with a parenthetical line reference
   ("'this' (line 12) refers to -"). Our site has no line numbers, so our convention mirrors it exactly with
   "(paragraph N)" inside the stem: "The word 'surprisingly' (paragraph 3) ...", "In paragraph 2 (paragraph 2) ..." is
   NOT needed - just use the parenthetical form where NITE would put "(line N)". Do not use "According to the second
   paragraph" as the default retrieval stem. Mix stem formats as real sets do: slash-option blank fills, "'this'
   (paragraph 3) refers to -", "explains the use of the word '...'", analogy items, and long tangled overlapping
   options. No graph questions without a printed graph. Avoid facts dated after about 2020. No passage built
   backwards for a structure question; keys must not lift a passage sentence verbatim; distractors close to the key,
   not absolute straw men; chronology distractors not mere reorderings of the key. Israeli context where natural.
39. RULE 9 CORRECTED AGAIN (applies to paragraph and reading items): famous encyclopedia stories told in the
   "In 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind ..." template are a tell (Plato's cave, Fleming, Euler, Rosetta,
   Galileo, Napoleon's height, Columbus/flat earth, Einstein-Bohr, Darwin, Silent Spring, Voynich, Mallory).
   Real items are ARGUMENT or OPINION snippets on unusual subjects by little-known or invented writers (an essayist,
   a historian with a plain name, "Professor Shore"), invented dialogues between two people with modern Israeli
   names, or fictional countries - and some real content only as lightly held background. Safest pattern: an
   invented or unnamed writer arguing a view (opinions, not factual claims about real people). Fewer dates and
   figures; shorter and more abstract than an encyclopedia entry.
40. Keys must not copy a sentence of the text; they should turn on a fine distinction (necessary vs sufficient,
   "not necessarily", order of steps, near-identical options differing by one clause). Distractors are plausible
   misreadings close to the text - never nonsense, never real trivia as options. Frames like "The following appeared
   in an article about X:" introduce an OPINION, not a narrated history. No news-style recent facts.

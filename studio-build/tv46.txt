# Topic 46 Strengthening and weakening arguments

######## SECTION vr46-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-argument-method: kind=lesson title='Strengthen or weaken: find the gap'
[1] Separate the observation from the conclusion
  BOARD: ['Observed: repair requests increased.', 'Conclusion: the product became less reliable.', 'Gap: were more products in use?']
  SCRIPT: Repair requests increased, so the product must have become less reliable. The first part is an observation; the second is an interpretation. Ask what would have to be true for the observation to justify that interpretation. If twice as many products were in use, a larger number of repair requests might coexist with the same or a lower failure rate. We have found a gap. Do this before reading the options closely. A short paraphrase such as ‘more repairs means worse quality’ lets you see exactly what an added fact would need to support or challenge.
[2] Strengthening is not proving
  BOARD: ['Make the conclusion more credible.', 'Remove a plausible rival explanation.', 'Provide a relevant comparison.']
  SCRIPT: A strengthening fact need not prove the conclusion beyond doubt. It should make the argument more persuasive relative to what we knew before. If the number of products in use stayed constant, that removes one alternative explanation for more repair requests. Other explanations might remain. This is why the answer can be right without settling every imaginable question. Focus on the specific conclusion in the stem. A fact that praises the company or confirms an unrelated detail does not necessarily support the reliability claim. Ask which part of the gap the new fact addresses.
[3] Weakening leaves the observation intact
  BOARD: ['Accept the reported result.', 'Challenge the step from result to explanation.']
  SCRIPT: For a weakening question, usually accept the stated observation and challenge the inference drawn from it. If sales fell after a shop changed its hours, the fall is still a fact. A road closure that prevented customers reaching the shop gives another explanation for that fall. It weakens the claim that the hours caused it. We do not need to pretend sales rose instead. Some options directly contradict an explicit premise and therefore do not function as a useful explanation of the given situation. Look for information that can coexist with the observation while making the proposed conclusion less secure.
[4] Counts, rates and who was observed
  BOARD: ['More failures, but how many items?', 'Fewer complaints, but who could complain?', 'A survey of attendees misses nonattendees.']
  SCRIPT: Three checks are especially useful. First, a count needs a denominator when the conclusion is about a rate. Second, reports depend on whether people notice and report a problem. Third, a sample can exclude the very people whose experience matters. A meeting-time survey of attendees may miss those unable to attend. These are not automatic objections to every argument. Use them when the facts make them relevant. A correct weakening option should identify a concrete alternative route from the situation to the observed result, rather than merely say that all research is uncertain.
[5] Draw the rival arrows
  BOARD: ['A → B?', 'B → A?', 'C → both A and B?']
  SCRIPT: If the conclusion is causal, sketch its arrow. Does music improve concentration, or do people who already concentrate well choose music? Could a third factor explain both, such as having a quieter workspace? The alternatives correspond to reverse causation and a shared cause. An option strengthens the original account when it makes a rival less plausible, perhaps through a fair comparison or a sequence that rules out the reverse route. An option weakens it when it supplies a credible rival. Keep the measured outcome fixed while you compare these stories.
[6] Read the demand one last time
  BOARD: ['Strengthen? Weaken?', 'Which does NOT weaken?', 'Explain the finding?']
  SCRIPT: A fact can be relevant and still point in the wrong direction for the question. Circle the demand, especially when it contains ‘not.’ In a does-not-weaken question, three choices may raise real doubts and the answer may be neutral or supportive. In an explain-the-finding question, look for a mechanism that makes the reported result less puzzling. Do not answer a different question simply because an option sounds like a familiar criticism. Before choosing, say the effect in one line: ‘This removes the attendance explanation,’ or ‘This makes the sample less representative.’ That line should connect the option to this argument.
--- Q vr46-g001: After a bakery began opening earlier, its weekly sales fell. The owner concludes that the earlier opening caused the fall. Which fact most weakens this conclusion?
  CHOICES: ['The earlier hours were chosen to attract commuters.', 'Roadworks blocked the bakery’s entrance during the same weeks.', 'The bakery displayed its new hours clearly.', 'The owner counted sales in the same way in both periods.'] correct=2
=== VIDEO solve-vr46-g001: kind=solution title='A second change at the same time'
[1] Your question
  BOARD: []
  SCRIPT: After a bakery began opening earlier, its weekly sales fell. The owner concludes that the earlier opening caused the fall. Which fact most weakens this conclusion?
[2] Put the message in plain English
  BOARD: ['Roadworks blocked the bakery’s entrance during the same weeks.']
  SCRIPT: Blocked access can reduce sales independently of opening hours. The observation remains true, but the proposed cause is no longer the only plausible explanation. Name the observed fact and the proposed conclusion separately. Then ask what this new information changes in the step between them. We need a relevant shift in support, not a complete proof or an unrelated criticism of the person making the claim.
[3] Keep each answer tied to the text
  BOARD: ['Alternative cause']
  SCRIPT: “The bakery displayed its new hours clearly.” fails because clear hours do not explain the sales fall. “The owner counted sales in the same way in both periods.” fails because consistent measurement supports comparability. “The earlier hours were chosen to attract commuters.” fails because an intended benefit does not identify the cause of the observed decline. We have a reason for rejecting each alternative. That matters more than whether an answer repeats a familiar word from the passage. A strong distractor often borrows the vocabulary while changing the owner, direction or strength of the claim. Return to the exact part of the text that decides between the closest two options.
[4] Choose the matching answer
  BOARD: ['Choice 2', 'Roadworks blocked the bakery’s entrance during the same weeks.']
  SCRIPT: The answer is choice 2: Roadworks blocked the bakery’s entrance during the same weeks.. Blocked access can reduce sales independently of opening hours. The observation remains true, but the proposed cause is no longer the only plausible explanation.

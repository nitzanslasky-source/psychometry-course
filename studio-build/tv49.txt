# Topic 49 Reading comprehension

######## SECTION vr49-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-reading-strategy: kind=lesson title='Reading comprehension: build a map, then use the evidence'
[1] A passage is a sequence of moves
  BOARD: ['What is the subject?', 'What question or disagreement drives the passage?', 'What does each paragraph add?']
  SCRIPT: A reading passage is more than a long collection of facts. A writer is usually moving through a question: introducing a puzzle, describing an explanation, testing it, then qualifying the conclusion. Start by looking for that movement. Our Active Reading habits now work at paragraph scale. Give each paragraph a short label and keep the owner of each opinion clear. You do not need to memorize every name, date or technical term on the first read. You need enough of a map to know where information belongs and how the writer’s main point develops.
[2] Two routes, one goal
  BOARD: ['Route A: read and map, then answer.', 'Route B: local questions first, reading enough context.', 'Try both on timed sets; compare accuracy as well as speed.']
  SCRIPT: One useful route is to start with local questions and read the relevant parts as you go. Another is to make a light map of the passage first. In English, a short first read with paragraph labels may be more efficient for you, especially when the passage builds a single argument. Try both routes on comparable practice sets. Count not only the time, but also the rereading and the mistakes. A strategy that seems fast until you miss the central contrast is not saving you much. Choose the route that reliably gives you enough context for the questions in front of you.
[3] Route A: read with a light map
  BOARD: ['After each sentence: a tiny paraphrase', 'After each paragraph: a short role label', 'No detailed summary of every fact']
  SCRIPT: For the read-first route, move through the passage once with a light touch. Pause briefly after a difficult sentence to put it in your own words. At the end of a paragraph, name its job: old account, new test, competing explanation, or limitation. Do not turn this into writing a separate summary of every sentence. The map should cost little time. When the questions begin, use it to return to the right area. A detail question still deserves a reread of the relevant text; the first reading is not a memory competition.
[4] Route B: local questions first
  BOARD: ['Find the first local demand.', 'Read the surrounding sentence or paragraph.', 'Build the map while answering.', 'Return to global questions.']
  SCRIPT: For the question-first route, begin with a demand that points to a particular phrase, paragraph or detail. Read enough surrounding text to understand it. A pronoun may require the previous sentence, and a contrast may require the start of the paragraph. As you answer, keep a growing map of what each part does. Leave a question about the whole passage until you have seen enough of its structure. Before answering that global question, fill any important gap in your reading. The method is selective reading with context, not grabbing isolated words and hoping the answers explain the passage for you.
[5] Do not turn order into a guarantee
  BOARD: ['Local questions often move through the text.', 'Use that as a search clue, not a rule.', 'According to the passage can introduce a local question too.']
  SCRIPT: Question order can help you look for a detail, but it is not a guarantee that every answer lies after the previous one. Likewise, the phrase ‘according to the passage’ does not automatically make a question global in English. Look at what it asks. A question about a named experiment may be local even with that opening. A question about the writer’s overall purpose is global even without it. The demand, rather than a single stock phrase, tells you how much of the passage you need. Use the wording as a clue, then check what information the answer actually requires.
[6] Read around the reference
  BOARD: ['“This result” → which result?', '“However” → contrast with what?', 'A definition may be one sentence earlier.']
  SCRIPT: When a question points to a phrase, read a little before and after it. ‘This result’ cannot be understood without the result it names. ‘However’ tells us that something turns, so we need the earlier claim as well as the sentence after it. A technical term may have been defined just before the quoted line. Our course uses paragraph numbers so references remain stable when the page size changes or the passage is printed. The exam may use line numbers. The skill is the same: use the reference as an entry point, then read enough context to recover the meaning.
[7] A meaning-in-context question
  BOARD: ['Use the meaning that fits this sentence.', 'Replace the word and reread.', 'Do not choose a dictionary sense that changes the point.']
  SCRIPT: A familiar word can have a less familiar sense in a passage. ‘Account’ might mean an explanation rather than a bank account. ‘Qualified’ might mean limited by reservations rather than professionally trained. Read the whole sentence, replace the word with your proposed meaning, and see whether the paragraph still makes sense. The best answer preserves both grammar and the writer’s point. A distractor may be a perfectly valid dictionary definition in another context. We reject it here because the neighboring ideas select a different sense.
[8] Inference stays close to the text
  BOARD: ['Combine stated information.', 'Keep its limits.', 'Do not import outside knowledge.']
  SCRIPT: An inference may not appear as one exact sentence, but its support should be visible. If a passage says a method works under one condition and not another, we can infer that the condition matters to its success. We cannot infer the exact cause unless the passage supplies it. Use the passage’s own definitions and invented study facts, even if you know a related real-world topic. Outside knowledge can make a plausible distractor feel true, but the question asks what this text supports. Point to the two or three statements that do the work.
[9] Purpose is different from content
  BOARD: ['Content: what does the example describe?', 'Purpose: why is it here?']
  SCRIPT: Suppose a paragraph describes a museum visitor touching a replica. The content is that action. The purpose may be to show how replicas allow an experience that preservation rules restrict for originals. A question asking why the example is included needs the second answer. Look at the claim the example follows and the conclusion the writer draws from it. Your paragraph labels help here. An option can accurately describe the scene while missing its argumentative job. Ask what would be harder to understand or support if the example were removed.
[10] Choose a title with the right scope
  BOARD: ['Too narrow: one example only', 'Too broad: a claim the passage never makes', 'Best fit: subject plus main development']
  SCRIPT: A title should cover the passage’s central development, not merely its most memorable example. If a passage begins with an old view and then explains why it needs qualification, a title that states only the old view misses the point. A dramatic universal claim may be too broad. Try a simple phrase joining the subject and the main idea: ‘When observation changes performance,’ or ‘Why an archive’s silence needs interpretation.’ Then compare the options with that phrase. The best title need not mention every detail, but it should not misrepresent the writer’s conclusion.
[11] A full check for a negative question
  BOARD: ['Supported', 'Contradicted', 'Not established']
  SCRIPT: For a question asking which statement is not supported, inspect all four options. Three may closely paraphrase the passage. The remaining one may be contradicted, or it may add a claim the passage leaves open. Keep those categories separate. If the demand says ‘contradicts,’ an unsupported possibility is not enough. If it says ‘does not follow,’ an extra unproved claim can be the answer. Circle the negative word before you start. Under time pressure, this tiny mark helps prevent answering the opposite question after you have done all the reading correctly.
[12] Budget the set, not only one question
  BOARD: ['Reading time serves several questions.', 'Skip a stubborn detail temporarily.', 'Return with a clear location to check.']
  SCRIPT: A passage and its questions form a set. Reading time is shared across that set, so a rigid one-minute rule for each question can be misleading. Practice with a realistic total allowance based on the paper format you are using, then look at where the time went. If one vocabulary detail is holding you up, mark it and continue when the other questions can still be answered. Do not lose the whole set trying to force certainty from one phrase. On review, record whether the problem was vocabulary, a missing connection, a scope error, or simply searching in the wrong paragraph.
[13] Use your review to improve the next read
  BOARD: ['Where did my paraphrase change the claim?', 'Which paragraph role did I miss?', 'What made the distractor attractive?']
  SCRIPT: After a practice set, do more than note the right letter. Find the exact point where your reading diverged from the text. Perhaps a reported opinion became the writer’s view. Perhaps ‘may’ became ‘must.’ Perhaps you remembered an example but forgot that the writer used it to challenge a general rule. Write a short correction that you can use on the next passage. That is how Active Reading becomes a practical skill rather than a list of techniques. The aim is a clear first understanding, followed by focused returns to evidence when the question needs them.

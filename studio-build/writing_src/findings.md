**What the official writing-task examples teach us**

Conclusions from the saved study of 33 English sample essays in 11 exams, from July 2013 to Summer 2024.

The exam papers label one essay in each set **Strong**, one **Adequate**, and one **Weak**. “Adequate” is the category referred to here as medium. The labels are official; the explanations below are my analysis, checked against the supplied official English writing guide. The papers do not provide the individual content and language scores for these essays, so Strong must not be converted into an assumed 6 and 6, or Adequate into an assumed 4 and 4.

These are qualitative patterns in the examples, not measured frequencies or proof that one particular feature caused a particular rating. The English examples are the basis of this study; the Hebrew examples are not included in the count of 33.

The main finding is that strong writing makes the reasoning easy to follow. A medium essay often starts with a relevant idea but leaves part of the explanation for the reader to supply. A weak essay may also contain a good idea, but lose the exact question, contradict its stated answer, or express the reasoning so unclearly that the reader cannot reconstruct it reliably.

| Feature | Strong examples | Adequate examples | Weak examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answering the task | Usually stay with the exact decision and relevant groups | Generally relevant, with loose comparisons or drifting passages | Often change the decision, population, or setting |
| Position | An identifiable answer supported through the essay | An answer is present, but some reasons are thin or poorly aligned | The stated answer and the reasons can point in different directions |
| Development | Explain how a change could lead to a consequence | Name useful consequences, then repeat or assume the connection | List claims, tell stories, or jump between ideas |
| Other views | Engage with a real competing reason or limitation | Mention opposition but answer it incompletely | Dismiss, misunderstand, or fail to address the competing concern |
| Organization | Paragraphs contribute to an overall argument | A workable outline with uneven space or repeated points | The direction of the essay becomes hard to follow |
| Language | Generally makes distinctions and relationships clear | Usually understandable, but inaccuracies reduce control | Sentence and word-choice problems can obscure the position itself |

**1. Answering the exact question is the first major requirement.**

An essay can sound thoughtful and still discuss the wrong issue. Summer 2022 concerns healthy children expected to be very short; discussing treatment for a hormone deficiency changes the population. Summer 2024 concerns how candidates are selected within a party; discussing a general election changes the process. These are reasoning problems, not small wording problems. Before generating reasons, students should identify the decision, the people affected, and the conditions that must remain true.

**2. Every part of the question needs an answer.**

The February 2016 task asks about benefits of competitive sport for individuals and society. A clear explanation of personal benefits alone leaves part of the task unfinished. The July 2014 task asks for a comparison using social and environmental values. A paragraph about pleasant scenery does not cover both. The outline should come from what the question asks, not from a memorized essay shape.

**3. A clear opinion at the beginning is not enough.**

The reasons must actually support it. In the July 2013 weak response, the stated support for abolishing the law is not clearly connected to the reasons about kind treatment and support for students. A reader should be able to follow the essay’s answer from its opening through its body to its conclusion. If the reasons point toward keeping a policy, the writer cannot simply end by calling for its abolition.

**4. Having a good idea and writing a good argument are different skills.**

The July 2018 weak essay contains a potentially useful affordability concern. That concern does not become a strong argument because it is not fully developed and the language makes it difficult to follow. We should not teach students that weak writers have no ideas. Often they need help turning an idea into a clear explanation.

**5. Development means adding something, not saying the same thing differently.**

The July 2018 adequate response repeatedly returns to personal attention and customer experience. These points are relevant, but several versions of “the service is better” do not automatically deepen the argument. A new sentence should explain a cause, identify a consequence, make a distinction, illustrate something useful, or state a meaningful limit. If it does none of those jobs, it may be repetition.

**6. The missing link is often the most useful thing to repair.**

“This policy will improve motivation” names an outcome. It does not explain how the policy produces it. Stronger passages tend to supply those intermediate steps. In July 2013, the strong essay explains how expelling a difficult student can move a problem outside school rather than solve it. In July 2018, the strong essay follows the possible effects of spending through the local community. These passages give the reader a route from the policy to the claimed result.

**7. Your chain method fits this need well, provided we test the arrows.**

The short chain is a planning tool: it helps students remember the steps they must explain. It is not an official marking requirement, and the exam examples do not prove that using arrows alone raises a score.

Your working-from-home example is a useful starting point. But each arrow needs a question: why would that step follow? Feeling trusted does not automatically mean trusting the company in return. Nor does feeling part of a company always produce greater effort. We need to explain the connection and include any important condition.

A possible teaching draft is: **flexibility → felt trust → ownership of agreed work → greater effort**. The paragraph would explain why those links may hold, while acknowledging the need for clear expectations and communication. This is an original teaching illustration, not a quotation from an exam.

Around ten words can be enough to remind a student of a reason. It should not become a fixed word limit. Keep the notes short, but do not drop a necessary link merely to make the chain shorter. Equally, do not add arrows that only rename the same outcome: “want to work harder” and “greater motivation” may be two ways of saying nearly the same thing.

**8. A second paragraph should add a different reason.**

Changing the people or the wording does not necessarily create a new argument. Several observations about comfort and friendly service may belong to one service argument. In the 2023 adequate response, the supposed second reason overlaps with the first. Students should ask what the next paragraph adds to the reader’s judgment. Two developed, distinct reasons can be a useful beginner plan, but the guide does not require exactly two.

**9. Stronger arguments often trace consequences beyond the most obvious person.**

The 2023 strong response discusses prisoners and their families, particularly children. The 2018 strong response looks beyond the customer’s immediate purchase to local businesses and the community. This is a useful way to find depth. It is not an instruction to mention every possible group. Each group should contribute a relevant, explained consequence.

**10. Comparisons need a fair basis.**

In the 2019 construction task, comparing the effects of one large building with one small building may miss how many people each houses. The useful comparison may be what is required to accommodate the same population. In 2014, living near nature is not automatically the same as using land or resources responsibly. Students should identify the measure that actually helps answer the question.

**11. Some exact words carry the argument.**

“Suspect” and “convicted offender” are different statuses. “Healthy” changes the 2022 medical-policy question. “Complete” changes the 2019 construction ban. “Right” and “privilege” imply different powers to withdraw contact in 2023. Repeating the correct term is better than choosing an inaccurate synonym for variety. Vocabulary accuracy is both a language skill and a content skill.

**12. Mentioning the other side is not the same as answering it.**

A paragraph that says “Some people disagree, but I still think my view is better” does very little. In 2021, the adequate response identifies an access problem but does not adequately confront the costs and uncertainty of developing drugs. A stronger response would explain why the competing concern does or does not justify the policy being considered. Students need to identify the other side’s reason, not merely its conclusion.

**13. The opposing argument should be treated fairly.**

It is easier to reject a claim after making it sound selfish or foolish, but that does not demonstrate much critical thought. The 2024 strong response takes the practical problems of primaries seriously. The 2015 strong response recognizes an interest in public information and safety. A student can disagree firmly without pretending the other side has no reasonable concern.

**14. A reply should target the actual problem.**

The 2023 strong response connects continued contact with rehabilitation and future conduct, addressing part of the concern behind discipline. This is more useful than merely repeating that contact matters. The teaching question is: “What does the opposing argument worry will happen, and how does this reply address that?” A safeguard or alternative only helps if its operation is explained.

**15. A qualified position can work, but its boundary must be coherent.**

“Both sides have a point” is not a complete answer. In 2019, supporting a complete ban while quietly allowing exceptions changes the proposal. A writer can reject the complete ban and defend a narrower policy, but must say that clearly. In 2023, a writer must explain how any exception fits the claimed status of contact as a right or privilege. A middle position has no automatic scoring advantage.

**16. The strength of the wording should match the support.**

One plausible consequence does not establish what will always happen. In 2022, exaggerated predictions about very tall children do not fit the modest change described in the prompt. In 2021, an assertion that every company will recover its costs within a particular number of years needs support. Words such as “may” or “can” can accurately express a possibility, but they do not repair an implausible explanation. “Many” and “most” also make claims that need care.

**17. Facts, assumptions, and reported opinions need to stay separate.**

The fact that the prompt reports what supporters believe does not prove their prediction. The 2013 strong response usefully questions whether disciplinary problems occurring alongside a law show that the law caused them. Students should ask whether a point is given, argued, predicted, or assumed. Critical thinking includes checking the link between the evidence and the conclusion.

**18. Examples should explain the reason, not replace it.**

The 2016 adequate essay relies heavily on personal sporting and viewing experiences. A story may make an idea easy to picture, but the essay still needs to explain what it demonstrates and why it matters beyond that experience. The official guide discourages a personal or narrative style. For teaching, a short, clearly hypothetical illustration can help students explain a process without turning the essay into autobiography.

**19. An example is not proof of how common something is.**

Showing how a policy could harm one person helps explain a possible mechanism. It does not establish that most people will experience that harm. Similarly, a comparison is useful only when the shared feature matters and relevant differences are acknowledged. Strong examples still contain claims that should be questioned; their label does not verify every factual statement.

**20. Specialist knowledge and invented evidence are unnecessary.**

The guide explicitly warns against incorrect or fabricated information. Students should not make up studies, statistics, expert opinions, or exact thresholds to make an argument sound authoritative. They can use accurate, relevant knowledge they actually have. They can also produce a useful argument by working carefully with the prompt and explaining plausible consequences. This is guidance from the official document, not an inference that every published sample follows it perfectly.

**21. The introduction should leave room for the argument.**

The 2019 adequate example spends considerable space on an opening close to the prompt. That reduces space for independent development. The practical lesson is to establish the issue and the answer efficiently. A quotation, a dramatic hook, or “Since the beginning of humanity…” is not required. The introduction still matters because it tells the reader what the essay is trying to establish.

**22. The conclusion should follow from the body.**

A closing statement cannot fix reasons that support a different position. Nor should the conclusion introduce a substantial new claim that the essay has not explained. It should give the judgment reached through the preceding argument. An unfinished ending can expose a time-management or completion problem, but a polished ending alone cannot rescue an unfocused essay.

**23. A fixed five-paragraph template is a teaching aid, not an exam rule.**

The 2016 benefits task is a clear reason to avoid forcing every response into a yes-or-no debate. The 2017 strong essay shows that an opposing consideration can appear early. The guide does not mandate five paragraphs, exactly two supporting reasons, a separate counterargument paragraph, or an example in every paragraph. Structure should help the writer answer the task and make the reasoning visible.

**24. Organization is about the jobs of paragraphs, not their count.**

An essay may have an introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion while repeating one point throughout. Conversely, a different arrangement can work if its direction is clear. Give each planned paragraph a short description of what it must establish. Then check whether the paragraph actually does that job. This is a more useful test than simply counting paragraphs or linking phrases.

**25. Difficult vocabulary is not a substitute for precise English.**

The guide values a rich vocabulary and useful range of sentence structures, but explicitly warns against needlessly difficult language. The goal is not to keep every sentence extremely basic. It is to use the range the student can control. “Because,” “but,” and “if” can express the relationship perfectly well. A more formal word is useful only when it conveys the intended meaning accurately and suits the sentence.

**26. Sentence errors matter especially when they alter the reasoning.**

Unclear pronouns, missing negatives, run-on sentences, incomplete sentences, and inaccurate role labels can make the reader misunderstand who acts, what causes what, or which policy the writer supports. Grammar work should use real argument sentences, so students see the connection between correctness and meaning. Correctness, clarity, precision, and variety remain language criteria in their own right; content development does not make language irrelevant.

**27. Connectors signal a relationship; they do not create one.**

Putting “therefore” before a conclusion does not establish that it follows. Putting “however” between contradictory positions does not explain a defensible exception. Teach students to identify the relationship first: cause, consequence, contrast, concession, condition, or example. Then choose the connector that accurately expresses it.

**28. Emotion and moral confidence cannot replace explanation.**

Several weaker or adequate passages rely on fear, indignation, admiration, or pressure on the reader. These may show that the writer cares, but the question still needs a reasoned answer. This does not mean an essay cannot discuss values, rights, harm, or fairness. It means the writer must explain who is affected, how, and why that should influence the decision. The teacher can speak warmly and naturally while the written essay remains measured.

**29. Strong essays are useful benchmarks, not flawless texts.**

Some contain spelling mistakes, broad claims, emotionally charged wording, or debatable qualifications. The official guide tells raters to assess a timed first draft. We should learn from a strong essay’s overall reasoning and organization while still finding sentences worth improving. We cannot conclude that a particular error is always ignored, or that a student is safe to imitate it.

**30. Length alone does not distinguish the levels.**

Some short responses are visibly underdeveloped or unfinished. But the longer weak examples also show that more writing can mean more drift rather than more explanation. The rules remain 25–50 handwritten lines in 35 minutes. The guide says good responses commonly occupy about 30–40 lines in average handwriting; that is guidance, not a different requirement or a universal word count. We should not derive an ideal word count from these samples.

**31. No preferred opinion or clever-looking format guarantees a strong result.**

We cannot infer from an official strong example that its political or moral position is the position the examiner wants. The guide explicitly allows different ideas when they are well reasoned and clearly supported. Likewise, we cannot infer a bonus for preserving the status quo, choosing a compromise, using a quotation, naming a right, or inventing an unusual psychological argument. These choices have to serve this question.

**32. Feedback should identify the obstacle to this essay’s clarity.**

My teaching recommendation is to check the exact task and the answer first, then reason-to-position alignment, missing explanation, repetition, and the treatment of the competing view. Alongside that, identify language problems that obscure the meaning. Do not begin every weak essay by replacing ordinary words with more advanced vocabulary. This is a practical feedback sequence, not a separate official scoring formula.

**33. The strongest teaching progression moves from small skills to a whole timed essay.**

Start with the actual page, instructions, answer sheet, and grading tables. Then practise one-line task summaries, clear answers, short reason chains, developed paragraphs, and responses to objections. Teach introductions and conclusions as parts of that argument. Finally combine the skills under timed conditions. A lesson should have a specific outcome the student can demonstrate, such as repairing a missing causal link or writing a reply that addresses the objection.

**34. Students should diagnose samples before seeing their labels.**

First read the full prompt. Then identify the essay’s answer, explain a strength or weakness with a specific passage, and suggest a repair. Reveal the official label afterward. Return to the whole essay before making an overall judgment: an isolated sentence cannot establish the essay’s level. Keep some prompts unseen for later practice, so improvement is not confused with remembering a model.

**35. Preserve the timed attempt, and test whether a repair transfers.**

Keep the student’s original essay separate from the improved version. After a short targeted exercise, ask the student to use the same skill on a fresh question. Improving a familiar essay with help is worthwhile, but it is not yet evidence that the student can do it independently in 35 minutes. This is a teaching recommendation drawn from the difficulties the examples expose, not an official numerical readiness threshold.

# Topic 44 Understanding paragraphs

######## SECTION vr44-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-paragraph-method: kind=lesson title='Understanding paragraphs: evidence, purpose and scope'
[1] Ask what each sentence is doing
  BOARD: ['Claim → reason → example → qualification', 'A topic is not the same as a purpose.']
  SCRIPT: A paragraph about a railway may describe its construction, defend a decision to build it, or challenge a popular explanation of its failure. ‘Railway’ is the topic in all three cases. The paragraph’s purpose is different. After each sentence, give it a small job label: claim, reason, example, opposing view, or qualification. You do not need all five labels in every paragraph. The point is to understand how the thought develops. A question asking why an example is mentioned wants that job, not just a description of what the example contains.
[2] Literal information and inference
  BOARD: ['Stated: find the matching information.', 'Implied: connect what the text actually supplies.', 'Neither: do not add a missing premise.']
  SCRIPT: Some questions ask what is stated. Others ask what follows or is implied. For a stated-information question, find the relevant sentence and compare the option carefully. An inference may combine two statements, but it still needs support. If a paragraph says a device is cheaper to produce and more expensive to maintain, it does not tell us which device costs less over ten years without more information. Do not confuse a plausible answer with an entailed one. The safest inference often sounds less exciting than the distractors because it respects what remains unknown.
[3] Keep two views in separate boxes
  BOARD: ['Writer A: what changes?', 'Writer B: what changes?', 'Disagreement: the exact difference']
  SCRIPT: When two thinkers are presented, use separate boxes or labels. They may agree on an observation and disagree only on its explanation. Or they may agree that a policy has a benefit while disagreeing about its importance. Ask what each person would accept and what each would reject. A broad answer such as ‘they disagree about science’ is rarely enough. We want the precise point of disagreement. The same habit helps with a writer who quotes a critic: the critic’s words belong in the critic’s box unless the writer explicitly endorses them.
[4] An example can test a boundary
  BOARD: ['General claim', 'Specific case', 'Does the case support, limit or challenge it?']
  SCRIPT: Examples are not decorative. A paragraph may use one to make an abstract idea concrete, show an exception, or expose a weakness in an argument. Read the sentence before and after the example. If the writer says ‘yet this account cannot explain...’ the example is a challenge. If the writer says ‘for instance,’ it is likely an illustration, but still check what feature is being illustrated. A distractor may accurately describe the example and still give the wrong reason for including it. The question is about its role in the paragraph’s reasoning.
[5] Unknown terms can have clear roles
  BOARD: ['A defined term gets its meaning from the paragraph.', 'Do not substitute a familiar but different meaning.']
  SCRIPT: A paragraph may introduce a technical term or an unfamiliar artistic movement. Read the definition supplied there. You usually do not need outside knowledge. If the text says a technique combines ordinary speech sounds to imitate an action, use that description even if you know a related term from another context. In a language-based example, pay attention to the specific feature the definition names. What matters is the same reading task: understand a supplied definition, recognize a case that fits it, and reject a superficially similar case that lacks the defining feature.
[6] Check scope and the negative demand
  BOARD: ['According to the paragraph...', 'Which is NOT supported?', 'Some, usually, only, may']
  SCRIPT: A negative question deserves a small visible mark. If it asks which statement is not supported, three options may be true paraphrases. Do not choose the first true-looking statement out of habit. Beside each answer, mentally put supported, contradicted, or not established. A not-supported answer may be false or simply go beyond the evidence; read the exact demand. Keep the paragraph’s scope: a finding in one setting is not automatically a rule for every setting. At the end, point to the sentence that supports your choice, or the precise extra claim that the text never supplied.
--- Q vr44-g001: The painter sought ordinary subjects rather than heroic ones. Yet she rearranged their settings to emphasize social conflict, instead of recording every visible detail exactly. Which statement best describes her approach?
  CHOICES: ['She treated exact visual copying as her only aim.', 'She believed that all ordinary scenes lacked social meaning.', 'She used ordinary subjects but shaped their presentation to express an idea.', 'She avoided ordinary life entirely.'] correct=3
=== VIDEO solve-vr44-g001: kind=solution title='Observation and artistic choice'
[1] Your question
  BOARD: []
  SCRIPT: The painter sought ordinary subjects rather than heroic ones. Yet she rearranged their settings to emphasize social conflict, instead of recording every visible detail exactly. Which statement best describes her approach?
[2] Put the message in plain English
  BOARD: ['She used ordinary subjects but shaped their presentation to express an idea.']
  SCRIPT: Ordinary subject matter and selective presentation are separate features. The paragraph accepts the first and describes deliberate choices in the second. Give the paragraph a short label before testing the answers. Then check whether the option describes the whole point or only repeats a detail. An accurate paraphrase should preserve both what the writer accepts and what the writer limits.
[3] Keep each answer tied to the text
  BOARD: ['Two dimensions']
  SCRIPT: “She avoided ordinary life entirely.” fails because ordinary subjects are explicitly her preference. “She treated exact visual copying as her only aim.” fails because rearranging settings shows that exact copying was not the sole aim. “She believed that all ordinary scenes lacked social meaning.” fails because the rearrangement is intended to bring out social meaning. We have a reason for rejecting each alternative. That matters more than whether an answer repeats a familiar word from the passage. A strong distractor often borrows the vocabulary while changing the owner, direction or strength of the claim. Return to the exact part of the text that decides between the closest two options.
[4] Choose the matching answer
  BOARD: ['Choice 3', 'She used ordinary subjects but shaped their presentation to express an idea.']
  SCRIPT: The answer is choice 3: She used ordinary subjects but shaped their presentation to express an idea.. Ordinary subject matter and selective presentation are separate features. The paragraph accepts the first and describes deliberate choices in the second.

  [on screen] Blank

  [board] $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=180°$
  SAYS: The sum of the angles in a triangle is 180. Alpha plus beta plus gamma equals 180.
  SAYS: I'm guessing most of you know this rule — and never thought about how to prove it. Let's do it quickly.
  (does: Through C, draw a line parallel to AB)

  (does: Draw a Z from B and copy β on the other side of C)
  SAYS: Same thing on the other side. Another Z — this one equals beta.
  SAYS: And now look along the new line: alpha, gamma, beta — they sit on one straight line.
  (does: Draw a half-circle arc over the three angles at C and write 180°)
  SAYS: A straight angle. 180. So the three angles of the triangle add up to 180.
  --- slide 7: Exterior angle
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v48.png - Triangle ABC]
  [board] Exterior angle $=$ the two interior angles not next to it
  SAYS: An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles that are not next to it.
  SAYS: First of all — what is an exterior angle?
  (does: Extend side AC past C, and mark the angle outside as γ)
  SAYS: It's the angle between one side of the triangle and the extension of another side.
  SAYS: It's outside the triangle — so it's exterior.
  SAYS: The rule says: this exterior angle equals alpha plus beta. We need to show gamma equals alpha plus beta. Let's prove it.
  (does: In the interior angle at C, write 180° − γ)
  SAYS: The angle inside is 180 minus gamma — they're adjacent angles, it completes it to 180.
  (does: Write α + β + (180° − γ) = 180°)
  SAYS: And by the angle sum: alpha plus beta plus 180 minus gamma is 180.
  (does: Cross out 180° on both sides; write α + β = γ)
  SAYS: Drop the parentheses, cancel 180 on the left and on the right.
  SAYS: We're left with alpha plus beta minus gamma equals zero. Move gamma across — alpha plus beta equals gamma.

  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v49.png - Triangle ABC]
  [board] Larger angle $\leftrightarrow$ longer side opposite it
  SAYS: In every triangle, the largest side lies opposite the largest angle.
  SAYS: And the smallest side lies opposite the smallest angle.

  SAYS: And it makes sense. Imagine opening an angle wider and wider — the side opposite it gets longer.

  --- slide 10: Two sides > third
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Any two sides together $>$ the third side
  [board] $4+6=10$
  SAYS: The sum of two sides of a triangle is always greater than the third side.
  SAYS: Let's understand what that means.
  SAYS: Suppose one side is 10. And the other two sides are 4 and 6 — exactly 10 together.
  (does: Draw a segment labelled 10; from its ends draw sticks 4 and 6 lifting up without meeting)
  SAYS: Watch what happens when I try to close a triangle. I lift the 4 to one side, the 6 to the other —
  SAYS: — and they don't reach. Lying flat, they're exactly 10. The moment they open up, a gap appears between them.
  SAYS: To close a triangle, I'd have to make one of them longer — or both.
  SAYS: So for this to be a triangle, the two sides together must be MORE than 10. Strictly more.
  --- slide 11: The shortest path
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v50.png - Triangle ABC]
  [board] $AB+BC>AC$
  SAYS: There's another way to see it.
  (does: Write 'home' by A and 'sports club' by C)

  SAYS: If I want to walk from home to the club — obviously the shortest way is to walk in a straight line.

  (does: Draw a second arrow from A to B and on to C)
  SAYS: The first walks straight. The second makes a stop at B on the way.
  SAYS: Who arrives first? The straight one — his road is shorter. The one who went through B walked more, and he's not there yet.
  SAYS: So two sides together are longer than the third — it's simply a longer route.

  --- slide 12: Between diff and sum
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $c<a+b$
  [board] $c+b>a$
  [board] $a-b<c<a+b$
  SAYS: Let's see an extension of this rule.
  SAYS: c is less than a plus b — two sides together are greater than the third.
  SAYS: But c and b are also two sides — so together they're greater than a.
  (does: Move b across: write c > a − b)
  SAYS: Move b to the other side — and we get c is greater than a minus b.
  SAYS: Put them together: a side of a triangle is less than the sum of the other two sides — and greater than their difference.
  (does: Circle the two '<' signs)
  SAYS: And both signs are strict — it can't be equal to either end.
  SAYS: For sides 7 and 12: more than 5 and less than 19. We'll use this in a moment.

### Q 2 (geo31-g011): In the accompanying figure, the angles at A and C are equal. What is the value of \(p+\frac{q}{2}\)?
  [FIGURE: fig/geo31-g011.png - Triangle ABC]
  (1) \(180°\)
  (2) \(90°\)
  (3) \(270°\)
  (4) \(360°\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Let each equal interior angle be u. Then p=180°−u and q=2u by the exterior-angle rule. Substitute: p+q divided by 2 equals 180°−u+u=180°.

### Q 4 (geo31-g014): ABC is an equilateral triangle. Point D lies on BC. Given: \(\angle DAC=30°\) and \(DC=6\) cm. What is AB (in cm)?
  [FIGURE: fig/geo31-g014.png - Angle bisector in an equilateral triangle]
  (1) \(18\)
  (2) \(24\)
  (3) \(12\)

  solution: Angle A is 60°, so AD bisects it into two 30° angles. In an equilateral triangle the angle bisector is also a median, so BD=DC=6. Hence BC=12 and AB=12.

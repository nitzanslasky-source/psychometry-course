### Q 1 (wp26-g093): A robot packs x samples in y minutes at a constant rate, where x and y are positive. How many samples will it pack in 4y minutes if its rate becomes 3 times as great?
  (1) \(3x\)
  (2) \(12x\)
  (3) \(7x\)
  (4) \(\frac{4x}{3}\)
  correct: 1
  solution: The robot originally packs x samples in y minutes. First keep those y minutes unchanged and increase only the rate. Three times the rate means three times as many samples in the same time, so its new output is 3x samples in y minutes.
  solution: Now keep that new rate and give the robot four times as long: 4y minutes instead of y. Each block of y minutes contributes another 3x samples. Four such blocks produce 12x samples. Both changes increase the output, so we multiply by both factors. We do not add three and four.
  solution: There are three useful relationships hiding in this example. With time fixed, increasing the rate increases the work by the same factor. With rate fixed, increasing the time increases the work by the same factor. With the amount of work fixed, increasing the rate reduces the time by the reciprocal factor. That last relationship is different because we are no longer asking for more work. We are finishing the same job sooner.
  solution: For a numerical picture, imagine x is six and y is two. The old robot packs six samples in two minutes. The faster robot packs eighteen in two minutes, and seventy-two in eight minutes. Seventy-two is twelve times six, just as the expression 12x predicts. These convenient values help us picture the relationship; the factor argument works for every positive x and y.

### Q 2 (wp26-g095): One inlet fills a tank in 8 hours. An identical inlet has the same rate. A drain empties a full tank in 12 hours. Starting with an empty tank at 09:00, both inlets and the drain run continuously. At what time is the tank full?
  (1) 15:00
  (2) 13:00
  (3) 14:00
  (4) 17:00
  correct: 0
  solution: Each inlet adds \(\frac18\) of a tank per hour. The second inlet adds another eighth. The drain removes \(\frac1{12}\) of a tank per hour, so its contribution has a minus sign. The net rate is \(\frac18+\frac18-\frac1{12}=\frac16\) of a tank per hour.
  solution: At one sixth of a tank each hour, filling one tank takes six hours. Starting at nine in the morning, six hours later is three in the afternoon, or 15:00. Notice that the requested answer is a clock time. Six hours is the duration, which must still be added to the starting time.
  solution: We cannot add eight, eight and twelve. Those are completion times for separate devices, and one device is working against the other two. Converting each device to a fraction of a tank per hour gives quantities that can sensibly be combined.

### Q 3 (wp26-g096): Machine A fills one order in 4 hours, while machine B fills the same order in 6 hours. If they work together at their usual constant rates, how many minutes do they need for one order?
  (1) 120
  (2) 150
  (3) 180
  (4) 144
  correct: 3
  solution: Use twelve hours, a common multiple of four and six. In twelve hours A completes three orders and B completes two. Together that is five orders in twelve hours. The time remains twelve hours: the machines are working alongside one another, so we add the orders, not the clock times.
  solution: One order so takes \(\frac{12}{5}\) hours. To turn hours into minutes, multiply by sixty. It is convenient to divide sixty by five first: twelve times twelve is 144 minutes. The fraction of an hour is not a decimal number of minutes.

### Q 4 (wp26-g098): Five identical workers make 30 display boards in 4 hours. How many hours do eight such workers need to make 72 display boards?
  (1) 9.6
  (2) 6
  (3) 4.8
  (4) 8
  correct: 1
  solution: We have three quantities to organise: workers, boards, and hours. Put them in that order in two rows. The first row says five workers make thirty boards in four hours. The second says eight workers make seventy-two boards in an unknown number of hours. Every worker has the same constant rate, so changing the team size changes the team’s rate in direct proportion.
  solution: Start with the four hours in the first row. If we keep the same five workers but change the work from thirty boards to seventy-two, the time increases by the factor seventy-two divided by thirty. That is twelve fifths. The larger job would take forty-eight fifths of an hour with the original team. We do not have to turn this into a decimal.
  solution: Now increase the team from five to eight workers. The job has not changed during this second step. More workers finish that fixed job sooner, so multiply the time by five eighths. The fives cancel, and forty-eight divided by eight is six. Eight workers need six hours.
  solution: It helps to say the direction aloud before calculating: more boards means more time; more workers means less time. Both numbers increased, but they push the time in opposite directions. Simply multiplying by both increases would make the extra workers slow the job down, which cannot fit this model.
  solution: The order of these two adjustments does not affect the final result. We could first ask how long eight workers need for thirty boards, then enlarge that job to seventy-two boards. Multiplication lets us combine the same two factors in either order. Choosing the order that cancels cleanly keeps the arithmetic short.

### Q 6 (wp26-g101): The combined rate of 3 high-speed scanners is twice the combined rate of 9 standard scanners. Scanners of the same type are identical. How many times as fast is one high-speed scanner as one standard scanner?
  (1) 6
  (2) 2
  (3) 3
  (4) 9
  correct: 0
  solution: Let F be one high-speed rate and S one standard rate. The three-machine team has rate 3F; the nine-machine team has rate 9S. The first is twice the second, so 3F=2×9S. Divide by three: F=6S. We multiply the smaller team-rate expression by two to make an equality, rather than doubling the already larger side.

### Q None (wp26-p23): Six workers complete a job in 14 days. After 4 days, two workers leave. How many additional days do the remaining workers need?
  (1) 12
  (2) 18
  (3) 15
  (4) 10
  correct: 2
  solution: The whole job requires 6×14=84 worker-days. The first four days provide 24, leaving sixty. Four workers need sixty divided by four, or fifteen more days.

### Q None (wp26-p24): Machine A makes 18 parts in 6 minutes. Machine B makes 20 parts in 5 minutes. Starting together, how many minutes do they need to make 84 parts altogether?
  (1) 14
  (2) 16
  (3) 12
  (4) 10
  correct: 2
  solution: A makes three parts per minute; B makes four. Together they make seven. Eighty-four parts so take twelve minutes.

### Q None (wp26-p25): A printer’s speed increases by 25%. A job previously took 50 minutes. How long does it now take?
  (1) 37.5
  (2) 45
  (3) 62.5
  (4) 40
  correct: 3
  solution: A 25% rise makes speed five quarters of the original. Time becomes four fifths as long. Four fifths of fifty minutes is forty minutes.

### Q None (wp26-p26): A can be emptied by drain A in 9 minutes or drain B in 18 minutes. Drain A works alone for 3 minutes; then B also opens. How many minutes after B opens is the can empty?
  (1) 6
  (2) 4
  (3) 3
  (4) 5
  correct: 1
  solution: A removes one third in the first three minutes, leaving two thirds. The combined rate is \(\frac19+\frac1{18}=\frac16\) can per minute. Time left is \(\frac23:\frac16=4\) minutes.

### Q None (wp26-p27): Eight identical machines produce 960 items in 3 hours. How many machines are needed to produce 1,600 items in 4 hours?
  (1) 10
  (2) 9
  (3) 12
  (4) 16
  correct: 0
  solution: One machine produces 960 divided by 24 machine-hours, or forty items per hour. In four hours it produces 160. To make 1,600 requires ten machines.

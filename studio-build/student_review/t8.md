  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 4³

  [board] Base $4$ · exponent $3$

  (does: Write "= 4 · 4 · 4")
  SAYS: Four cubed means three fours, multiplied together.
  (does: Write "= 64")
  SAYS: Sixty-four.
  (does: Draw an arrow to the 4 and an arrow to the 3)
  SAYS: The number at the bottom is the base. The small one up top is the exponent.
  SAYS: And it is NOT four times three. The exponent counts how many copies of the base you multiply.
  --- slide 3: Exponent 1 and 0
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $a^1=a$
  [board] $a^0=1\quad(a\ne0)$
  [board] $\frac{5^3}{5^3}$
  [board] $(-7)^0 \qquad \left(\frac{3}{7}\right)^0$
  SAYS: Any number to the power of one is itself. One copy of the base — that's it.
  SAYS: Any nonzero number to the power of zero is ONE. Sounds strange — so here's why.
  (does: Write "= 1"; then write "= 5³⁻³ = 5⁰")
  SAYS: A number over itself is one. But subtracting the exponents gives five to the zero. So five to the zero has to be one.
  (does: Write "= 1" after each one)
  SAYS: Negative base, fraction base — doesn't matter. The whole bracket to the zero: one.
  --- slide 4: Bases 1 and 0
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $1^n=1$
  [board] $0^n=0\quad(n>0)$
  [board] Zero to a negative power is undefined.
  SAYS: One to any power is one. One times one times one — a million times — still one.
  SAYS: Zero to a POSITIVE power is zero. Zero times anything is zero.
  (does: Underline "undefined")
  SAYS: A negative exponent means dividing by zero — undefined. Zero to the zero? Not defined in this course either.
  SAYS: You'll probably never meet those. Just know they're off-limits.
  --- slide 5: Negative exponents
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — (2/5)⁻³
  [board] $\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^{-3}$
  [board] $2^{-3}$
  SAYS: A negative exponent does NOT make the number negative.
  SAYS: It tells you: flip it.
  (does: Write "= (5/2)³")
  SAYS: Flip the fraction, and the minus in the exponent disappears.
  (does: Write "= 125/8")
  SAYS: Five cubed over two cubed: a hundred twenty-five eighths. Positive.
  (does: Write "= 1/2³ = 1/8")
  SAYS: A whole number? It's two over one — flip it. One over two cubed. One eighth.
  SAYS: The minus in the exponent means "flip". A minus in the base decides the sign. Keep them apart.
  --- slide 6: Multiplying powers
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 3² · 3⁴
  [board] $3^2\cdot3^4$
  [board] $3^2+3^4$
  SAYS: Same base, multiplied — ADD the exponents.
  (does: Under it write "(3·3)·(3·3·3·3)")
  SAYS: Why? Three squared is two threes. Three to the fourth is four more. That's six threes.
  (does: Write "= 3²⁺⁴ = 3⁶")
  SAYS: Three to the sixth. The base stays three.
  (does: Write "≠ 3⁶" and cross it out)
  SAYS: But this is a PLUS. The rule is for multiplying only. Nine plus eighty-one is ninety — nowhere near three to the sixth.
  SAYS: Same base AND a multiplication sign. Check both before you add exponents.
  --- slide 7: Dividing powers
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 5⁷ / 5³
  [board] $\frac{5^7}{5^3}$
  [board] $\frac{5^2}{5^5}$
  SAYS: Same base, divided — SUBTRACT the exponents. Top minus bottom.
  (does: Cross out three 5s on top against the three 5s on the bottom)
  SAYS: Seven fives on top, three on the bottom. Cancel three pairs.
  (does: Write "= 5⁷⁻³ = 5⁴")
  SAYS: Four fives are left. Five to the fourth.
  (does: Write "= 1/5³ = 5⁻³")
  SAYS: More on the bottom? The leftovers stay downstairs: one over five cubed — or five to the minus three.
  --- slide 8: Power of a power
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — (2³)⁴
  [board] $(2^3)^4$
  [board] $2^3\cdot2^4$
  SAYS: A power raised to a power — MULTIPLY the exponents.
  (does: Under it write "2³ · 2³ · 2³ · 2³")
  SAYS: Two cubed, four times over. Four groups of three twos.
  (does: Write "= 2³ˣ⁴ = 2¹²")
  SAYS: Twelve twos. Two to the twelfth.
  (does: Write "= 2⁷")
  SAYS: Careful — this one isn't a power of a power. It's multiplying: add. Two to the seventh.
  SAYS: Brackets around a power: multiply. Two powers side by side: add. Students mix these up — you won't.
  --- slide 9: Same exponent
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $(ab)^n=a^n b^n$
  [board] $(2\cdot3)^3$
  [board] $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n=\frac{a^n}{b^n}$
  [board] $(a+b)^2\ne a^2+b^2$
  SAYS: Different bases, same exponent? You can put them under ONE exponent.
  (does: Write "= 2³ · 3³ = 8 · 27 = 216")
  SAYS: Why? Two cubed times three cubed is three twos and three threes. Multiplication lets us reorder them into three pairs of two times three.
  SAYS: Two times three, cubed — or two cubed times three cubed. Same thing: two sixteen. You'll use this a LOT.
  SAYS: Division works the same way. The exponent goes on top and bottom.
  (does: Circle the + sign)
  SAYS: But NOT over a plus or a minus. The square of a sum has a middle term — we learned that.
  --- slide 10: Negative bases
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $(-3)^4$
  [board] $(-3)^3$
  [board] $-3^2$
  (does: Write "= 81")
  SAYS: A negative base to an EVEN power: the minuses pair off. Positive eighty-one.
  (does: Write "= −27")
  SAYS: An ODD power: one minus is left over. Negative twenty-seven.
  (does: Write "= −(3²) = −9")
  SAYS: No brackets? The power comes first — the minus comes after. Three squared is nine, then the minus: negative nine.
  SAYS: Brackets decide what the base is. Plugging in a negative number yourself? Always put it in brackets.
  --- slide 11: When aᵇ = 1
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $a^b=1$
  [board] Option 1:  $a=1$ — any $b$
  [board] Option 2:  $a=-1$ — $b$ must be even
  [board] Option 3:  $b=0$ — any $a\ne0$
  SAYS: A rule the exam loves. When is a to the b equal to one?
  SAYS: Option one: a is one. Then b can be anything.
  SAYS: Option two: a is negative one — but then b must be even.
  (does: Box all three options)
  SAYS: Option three: b is zero. Then a doesn't matter — anything nonzero to the zero is one.
  SAYS: When a question says a to the b equals one — check all three.
  --- slide 12: 2⁴ = 4²
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $2^4 \qquad 4^2$
  SAYS: Usually, swapping the base and the exponent changes the answer.
  (does: Write "= 16" under each one)
  SAYS: Two to the fourth: sixteen. Four squared: sixteen.
  SAYS: This pair — two and four — is the one special case. It's not a rule. It's an exception.
  SAYS: Any other pair of different numbers? One side is always bigger. Just remember two and four.
  --- slide 13: Splitting exponents
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2ⁿ⁺³
  [board] $2^{n+3}$
  [board] $2^{n+3}-2^n$
  SAYS: The multiplication rule works backwards too.
  (does: Write "= 2ⁿ · 2³ = 8 · 2ⁿ")

  (does: Write "= 8 · 2ⁿ − 1 · 2ⁿ = 7 · 2ⁿ")
  SAYS: Eight copies of two to the n, minus one copy. Seven copies.
  SAYS: We never subtracted exponents across a minus sign. We split first, then took a common factor.

### Q None (q-218): \({0}^{\sqrt{2}} + {\left(\sqrt{2}\right)}^{1} = ?\)
  (1) \(\sqrt{2}\)
  (2) \(0\)
  (3) \(\sqrt{2} + 1\)
  (4) \(1\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Zero raised to any positive power is 0, so 0^√2 = 0 . Any number to the first power is itself, so (√2)¹ = √2 . The sum is 0 + √2 = √2 .

### Q None (q-219): \({1}^{\sqrt{5}} + {\left(\sqrt{5}\right)}^{0} = ?\)
  (1) \(\sqrt{5}\)
  (2) \(\sqrt{5} + 1\)
  (3) \(2\)
  (4) \(1\)
  correct: 2
  solution: One raised to any power is 1, so 1^√5 = 1 . Any nonzero number to the zero power is 1, so (√5)⁰ = 1 . The sum is 1 + 1 = 2 .

### Q None (q-220): \(-{\left(-4\right)}^{3} = ?\)
  (1) \(-256\)
  (2) \(256\)
  (3) \(-64\)
  (4) \(64\)
  correct: 3
  solution: First compute the inner power: (−4)³ = (−4)·(−4)·(−4) = −64 , since an odd power keeps the negative sign. The leading minus flips it: −(−64) = 64 .

### Q None (q-221): \({\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)}^{-3} = ?\)
  (1) \(\frac{-125}{8}\)
  (2) \(\frac{125}{8}\)
  (3) \(\frac{-8}{125}\)
  (4) \(\frac{8}{125}\)
  correct: 1
  solution: A negative exponent flips the fraction: (2:5)⁻³ = (5:2)³ . Now cube top and bottom: 5³ = 125 and 2³ = 8 , giving 125:8 .

### Q None (q-222): \({\left(-3\right)}^{-4} = ?\)
  (1) \(-81\)
  (2) \(81\)
  (3) \(\frac{-1}{81}\)
  (4) \(\frac{1}{81}\)
  correct: 3
  solution: The negative exponent means reciprocal: (−3)⁻⁴ = 1:(−3)⁴ . An even power wipes out the sign: (−3)⁴ = 81 . So the value is 1:81 .

### Q None (q-223): \({11}^{-7} \cdot  {11}^{12} \cdot  {11}^{-3} = ?\)
  (1) \(11\)
  (2) \({11}^{3}\)
  (3) \(121\)
  (4) \(1\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Same base throughout, so add all the exponents: −7 + 12 − 3 = 2 . The product is 11² = 121 .

### Q None (q-224): \({5}^{x + 2} = ?\)
  (1) \(5 + {5}^{x}\)
  (2) \(5 \cdot  {5}^{x}\)
  (3) \(25 \cdot  {5}^{x}\)
  (4) \(25 + {5}^{x}\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Run the product rule in reverse: 5^(x+2) = 5^x · 5² = 25 · 5^x .

### Q None (q-225): \(\frac{{13}^{9}}{{13}^{7}} = ?\)
  (1) \({13}^{16}\)
  (2) \(\frac{1}{169}\)
  (3) \(169\)
  (4) \(13\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Dividing powers of the same base subtracts the exponents: 13^(9−7) = 13² = 169 .

### Q None (q-226): \(\frac{2 \cdot  {2}^{-6}}{{2}^{-10}} = ?\)
  (1) \({2}^{-15}\)
  (2) \(\frac{1}{32}\)
  (3) \(32\)
  (4) \(1\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Combine everything as one power of 2: the numerator is 2¹ · 2⁻⁶ = 2⁻⁵ , and dividing by 2⁻¹⁰ subtracts a negative: 2^(−5−(−10)) = 2⁵ = 32 .

### Q None (q-227): \({\left({7}^{3}\right)}^{\frac{2}{3}} = ?\)
  (1) \(49\)
  (2) \(7\)
  (3) \(343\)
  (4) \(2401\)
  correct: 0
  solution: A power of a power multiplies the exponents: 3 · (2:3) = 2 . So (7³)^(2:3) = 7² = 49 .

## Section: Additional exponent practice (practice)

### Q None (q-228): \({2}^{4} \cdot  {5}^{4} = ?\)
  (1) \(1{,}000{,}000\)
  (2) \(100{,}000\)
  (3) \(10{,}000\)
  (4) \(1{,}000\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Same exponent, different bases — so multiply the bases: 2⁴ · 5⁴ = (2 · 5)⁴ = 10⁴ = 10,000 .

### Q None (q-229): \(\frac{{6}^{4}}{{2}^{4}} = ?\)
  (1) \(1296\)
  (2) \(81\)
  (3) \(16\)
  (4) \(64\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Same exponent on top and bottom, so divide the bases first: 6⁴:2⁴ = (6:2)⁴ = 3⁴ = 81 .

### Q None (q-230): \(\frac{{5}^{3}}{{15}^{3}} = ?\)
  (1) \(\frac{1}{27}\)
  (2) \(\frac{1}{15}\)
  (3) \(\frac{1}{9}\)
  (4) \(\frac{1}{3}\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Divide the bases under the shared exponent: 5³:15³ = (5:15)³ = (1:3)³ = 1:27 .

### Q None (q-231): Given: \({a}^{b} = 1\). Which of the following is not possible?
  (1) \(a = 0\)
  (2) \(b = 7\)
  (3) \(b = 0\)
  (4) \(a = -1\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Check each option. b = 7 works if a = 1 , since 1⁷ = 1 . b = 0 works for any nonzero a, since a⁰ = 1 . a = −1 works with any even b, since (−1)² = 1 . But a = 0 can never give 1: 0 raised to a positive power is 0, and 0 to a zero or negative power is undefined.

### Q None (q-232): Let \(a\) and \(b\) be positive integers with \(a < b\) and \(a^b = b^a\). What is \(a + b\)?
  (1) \(4\)
  (2) \(8\)
  (3) \(6\)
  (4) \(2\)
  correct: 2
  solution: The classic pair of distinct positive numbers satisfying a^b = b^a is a = 2 , b = 4 : indeed 2⁴ = 16 and 4² = 16 . Since 0 < a < b forces a = 2 and b = 4 , the sum is a + b = 6 .

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-1): Evaluate \(\frac{2^{6}}{2^{3}}\).
  (1) 8
  (2) 2
  (3) 4
  (4) 16
  correct: 0
  solution: Subtract the denominator exponent from the numerator exponent: the difference is three. The result is 2 cubed, or 8.

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-2): Evaluate \(27^{\frac23}\).
  (1) 27
  (2) 9
  (3) 3
  (4) 6
  correct: 1
  solution: Take the cube root of 27 to get 3, then square it to get 9.

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-3): Solve \(3^{x+1}=3^{4}\).
  (1) 9
  (2) 3
  (3) 2
  (4) 4
  correct: 1
  solution: Equal powers of the positive base three have equal real exponents. x+1=4, so x=3.

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-4): Evaluate \(2^{-3}+2^{-2}\).
  (1) \(\frac{1}{32}\)
  (2) \(\frac{1}{8}\)
  (3) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
  (4) \(\frac{3}{8}\)
  correct: 3
  solution: The two terms are reciprocals of 8 and 4. Put them over denominator 8; the numerators become one and 2, giving 3 over 8.

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-5): For \(x\ne0\), simplify \(\frac{(2x)^3}{4x^2}\).
  (1) \(2x^2\)
  (2) \(x\)
  (3) \(8x\)
  (4) \(2x\)
  correct: 3
  solution: Expand the numerator to 8x cubed. Dividing coefficients leaves 2, and subtracting exponents leaves x.

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-6): Which is greater: \(2^{10}\) or \(4^{4}\)?
  (1) It cannot be determined from the information given.
  (2) The first power
  (3) The second power
  (4) They are equal
  correct: 1
  solution: The second power is two to the power 8. The first has exponent two larger, so it is greater.

### Q None (alg-extra-exponent-extra-7): Evaluate \(\sqrt{50}+\sqrt{8}\).
  (1) \(6\sqrt2\)
  (2) \(7\sqrt2\)
  (3) \(\sqrt{58}\)
  (4) \(8\sqrt2\)
  correct: 1

# Topic 2: Fractions — Fundamentals

## Section: 1 · What a fraction means (learn)

### VIDEO: What Is a Fraction? (6.1 min)
  --- slide 1: What Is a Fraction?
  [on screen] Massive title — "What Is a Fraction?"
  SAYS: Fractions. Another piece of the algebra foundations.
  SAYS: You'll need them for equations and expressions — and for word problems and geometry too.
  SAYS: So before any rules, let's get what a fraction actually IS.
  SAYS: Let's start.
  --- slide 2: Part of a whole
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Equal parts of the same whole
  SAYS: A fraction is a part of a whole.
  SAYS: Here's a whole pizza cut into four slices. One is shaded.
  (does: Next to the pie write "1/4")
  SAYS: One out of four. We write it one over four — one quarter.
  SAYS: Read that fraction bar as "out of."
  (does: Underline "Equal")
  SAYS: And one word is essential: EQUAL.
  SAYS: One giant slice and three tiny ones? Then one slice is NOT a quarter.
  SAYS: Whenever we cut a whole, we cut it into equal parts.
  --- slide 3: Parts of a fraction
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\frac{3}{4}$
  [board] Numerator — how many pieces we have
  [board] Denominator — how many equal pieces make one whole
  SAYS: Now shade three of the four slices.
  SAYS: Three quarters. Three copies of one quarter.
  SAYS: The number on top is the numerator. It counts the pieces we have.
  SAYS: The number underneath is the denominator. It tells you how many equal pieces make one whole.
  (does: Draw an arrow from each label to its number)
  SAYS: Watch the trap: the denominator is NOT the unshaded pieces.
  SAYS: There's one unshaded slice here — and the denominator is still four.
  SAYS: Top: numerator. Bottom: denominator.
  --- slide 4: Improper & mixed
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\frac{4}{4}=1 \qquad \frac{8}{4}=2$
  SAYS: Who says we can only count pieces from one whole?
  SAYS: Seven quarter-slices. The first four fill a whole pizza. Three more go into the next one.
  (does: Under the pies write "7/4")
  SAYS: Seven quarters — the numerator is bigger than the denominator. That's an improper fraction.
  SAYS: "Improper" is just a name. It's bigger than one — and it's perfectly correct.
  (does: Next to it write "= 1 3/4")
  SAYS: Same amount: one whole and three quarters. That's a mixed number — a whole number AND a fraction.
  SAYS: And that "and" means PLUS. One plus three quarters. Never times.
  SAYS: When top and bottom are equal, the fraction is exactly one. Eight quarters? Exactly two.
  --- slide 5: Improper → mixed
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 15/4
  [board] $\frac{15}{4}$
  [board] $\frac{a}{b}$ needs $b\ne0$
  SAYS: Improper to mixed: how many times does the denominator fit into the numerator? Four into fifteen?
  (does: Write "4 · 3 = 12")
  SAYS: Four fits in whole up to twelve — three times. That's the whole part.
  (does: Write "15 − 12 = 3")
  SAYS: Three left over. The remainder goes on top.
  (does: Write "= 3 3/4")
  SAYS: The denominator stays four. Three and three quarters.
  SAYS: Why does this work? Because a fraction bar IS division. Fifteen over four is fifteen divided by four.
  SAYS: And that's why a denominator can never be zero.
  --- slide 6: Mixed → improper
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2 3/5 (two and three fifths)
  [board] $2\frac{3}{5}$
  SAYS: And back the other way: mixed number to improper fraction.
  SAYS: Count everything in fifths.
  SAYS: Each whole holds five fifths. Two wholes: ten fifths.
  (does: Write "= (2 · 5 + 3) / 5")
  SAYS: Plus the three extra fifths.
  (does: Write "= 13/5")
  SAYS: Thirteen fifths.
  SAYS: The rule: whole number times denominator, plus numerator — over the SAME denominator.
  SAYS: The pieces didn't change size, so the denominator stays five.
  SAYS: Check it by going back: five into thirteen — two times, three left over. Two and three fifths.
  --- slide 7: Expanding
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\frac{3}{5}$
  SAYS: Take half a pizza.
  SAYS: Cut every slice in two. Then again.
  (does: Under the pies write "1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8")
  SAYS: Same shaded half — two quarters, then four eighths.
  SAYS: The amount never changed. Top and bottom both changed — by the same factor.
  SAYS: That's expanding: multiply the numerator AND the denominator by the same number.
  (does: Write "×4" on top and bottom, then "= 12/20")
  SAYS: Three fifths, times four on top and bottom: twelve twentieths.
  SAYS: Change only one of them? You've changed the amount. Two quarters is a half — but two halves is a whole.
  --- slide 8: Reducing
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 18/24
  [board] $\frac{18}{24}$
  SAYS: Reducing is the same move backwards: DIVIDE top and bottom by the same number.
  SAYS: Picture the pizza: erase some of the cut lines. Fewer, bigger slices — the same shaded amount.
  SAYS: Eighteen and twenty-four — both divide by six.
  (does: Write ":6" on top and bottom, then "= 3/4")
  SAYS: Three quarters.
  SAYS: Didn't spot the six? No problem — go in stages.
  (does: Below, write "18/24 = 9/12 = 3/4", with ":2" and then ":3")
  SAYS: Divide by two: nine twelfths. Divide by three: three quarters. Same place.
  SAYS: You're done when top and bottom share no common factor except one.
  SAYS: And an unreduced fraction isn't wrong. Sometimes you WANT the bigger denominator — to match another fraction.
  --- slide 9: Bigger or smaller?
  [on screen] Blank
  SAYS: What happens when we change the numbers?
  SAYS: Grow the numerator — more pieces — and the fraction grows.
  (does: Under the pies write "1/4 < 3/4")
  SAYS: One quarter, three quarters. More pizza. Easy.
  SAYS: Now the tricky one. Grow the denominator — and the fraction SHRINKS.
  SAYS: Same pizza, cut into six instead of four. Every slice gets smaller.
  (does: Under the new pies write "1/4 > 1/6")
  SAYS: One sixth is less than one quarter — even though six is bigger than four.
  SAYS: Think of cakes shared between people. The numerator is the cakes. The denominator is the people.
  SAYS: More cakes — everyone gets more. More people — everyone gets less.
  --- slide 10: What cancelling means
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — (6 · 5) over (6 · 7)
  [board] $\frac{6\cdot5}{6\cdot7}$
  [board] $\frac{6+5}{6\cdot7}$
  SAYS: You'll hear "cancel" constantly. Here's what it really means.
  SAYS: Divide the WHOLE top and the WHOLE bottom by the same number.
  (does: Cross out both 6s and write "= 5/7")
  SAYS: Both are six times something. Divide both by six: five sevenths.
  SAYS: But look at this one. Now the top is six PLUS five.
  (does: Draw a big ✗ over the 6 on top)
  SAYS: That six isn't a factor of the whole top. You can NOT cancel it.
  SAYS: Ask yourself: does it multiply the ENTIRE top and the ENTIRE bottom? If not — hands off.
  --- slide 11: Recap
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Numerator: how many pieces · Denominator: how many make a whole
  [board] Bigger numerator $\to$ bigger · Bigger denominator $\to$ smaller
  [board] Same factor on top and bottom $\to$ same value
  [board] Cancel factors — never terms in a sum
  SAYS: Let's lock it in.
  (does: Underline "smaller" in the second line)
  SAYS: This lesson is the foundation for every fraction you'll meet — in algebra, word problems and geometry.
  SAYS: Five questions next. Before you calculate, say what the numbers are counting.
  SAYS: Next: multiplying and dividing fractions.

### Q None (q-076): \(\frac{3}{4} = \frac{?}{24}\)
  (1) \(20\)
  (2) \(18\)
  (3) \(16\)
  (4) \(12\)
  correct: 1
  solution: The denominator went from 4 to 24 — multiplied by 6. So multiply the numerator by 6 as well: 3 · 6 = 18 .

### Q None (q-077): \(\frac{60}{240} = ?\)
  (1) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
  (2) \(\frac{1}{5}\)
  (3) \(\frac{1}{6}\)
  (4) \(\frac{1}{3}\)
  correct: 0
  solution: 240 : 60 = 4 , so 60 fits into 240 exactly four times: 60:240 = 1:4 .

### Q None (q-078): \(\frac{6}{15} = \frac{?}{10}\)
  (1) \(5\)
  (2) \(4\)
  (3) \(3\)
  (4) \(6\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Reduce first: 6:15 = 2:5 . Then expand to tenths: 2:5 = 4:10 , so the missing numerator is 4.

### Q None (q-079): \(\frac{29}{6} = ?\)
  (1) \(4\,\frac{1}{6}\)
  (2) \(5\,\frac{1}{6}\)
  (3) \(4\,\frac{5}{6}\)
  (4) \(5\,\frac{5}{6}\)
  correct: 2
  solution: 29 : 6 = 4 remainder 5, so 29:6 = 4 5:6 .

### Q None (q-080): \(7\,\frac{2}{5} = ?\)
  (1) \(\frac{37}{5}\)
  (2) \(\frac{39}{5}\)
  (3) \(\frac{35}{5}\)
  (4) \(\frac{36}{5}\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Mixed to improper: 7 · 5 + 2 = 37 , so 7 2:5 = 37:5 .

## Section: 2 · Multiplying and dividing (learn)

### VIDEO: Multiplying & Dividing Fractions (5.4 min)
  --- slide 1: Multiplying & Dividing Fractions
  [on screen] Massive title — "Multiplying & Dividing Fractions"
  SAYS: Multiplying and dividing fractions.
  SAYS: Good news: multiplying is the EASIEST fraction operation. Easier than adding.
  SAYS: And we'll use the reducing skills from the last lesson. If those still feel shaky — go back and practise first.
  --- slide 2: Multiplying
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{5}$
  [board] $\frac{2}{7}\cdot\frac{3}{5}$
  SAYS: What does three quarters OF two fifths even mean? Let's see it.
  SAYS: Two columns out of five: two fifths. Then three rows out of four: three quarters of that.
  (does: Circle the dark overlap and count the 6 small squares)
  SAYS: The overlap: six little pieces, out of twenty in the whole.
  (does: Write "= (3·2)/(4·5) = 6/20 = 3/10")
  SAYS: So: top times top, bottom times bottom. Six twentieths — reduce to three tenths.
  SAYS: No common denominator needed. That's only for adding.
  (does: Write "= 6/35")
  SAYS: Two sevenths times three fifths: six thirty-fifths.
  SAYS: Two fractions below one, multiplied — the answer is SMALLER. You're taking a part of a part.
  --- slide 3: Cancel first
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 14/15 · 25/21
  [board] $\frac{14}{15}\cdot\frac{25}{21}$
  SAYS: We could multiply fourteen by twenty-five… and fifteen by twenty-one… and then reduce some monster fraction.
  SAYS: Or: cancel FIRST.
  SAYS: Fourteen and twenty-one share a seven.
  (does: Cross out 14 and 21; write 2 and 3)
  SAYS: Two and three.
  SAYS: Twenty-five and fifteen share a five.
  (does: Cross out 25 and 15; write 5 and 3)
  SAYS: Five and three.
  (does: Write "= (2·5)/(3·3) = 10/9")
  SAYS: Ten ninths.
  SAYS: Straight down or diagonally across — it doesn't matter. Once you multiply, all the tops are one product and all the bottoms are another.
  SAYS: But ONLY with multiplication. A plus or a minus in there? No cancelling.
  SAYS: Didn't spot it early? No problem — you can still reduce at the end.
  --- slide 4: Whole numbers
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 6 · 7/12
  [board] $6\cdot\frac{7}{12}$
  SAYS: A whole number has no denominator… or does it?
  SAYS: Six is six over one. Six cakes for one person — six.
  (does: Write "= 6/1 · 7/12")
  SAYS: Now it's a normal fraction times a fraction.
  (does: Cross out 6 and 12; write 1 and 2; then write "= 7/2")
  SAYS: Six and twelve cancel to one and two. Seven halves.
  SAYS: Seven halves is bigger than one — improper. You can also write it as three and a half.
  --- slide 5: Use the reciprocal
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 3/4 : 2/5
  [board] $\frac{3}{4}:\frac{2}{5}$
  [board] $\frac{5}{6}:5$
  SAYS: Now the rule. To divide by a fraction — multiply by its reciprocal.
  SAYS: Keep the first fraction. Change divide to times. Flip the second.
  (does: Write "= 3/4 · 5/2")
  SAYS: Three quarters times five halves.
  (does: Write "= 15/8")
  SAYS: Fifteen eighths.
  SAYS: Which one do you flip? Only the one AFTER the division sign. Never the first.
  SAYS: Remember the name: multiply by the reciprocal. First you change to multiply — THEN you flip. So the flip lands on the second one.
  SAYS: Dividing by a whole number? Same rule. Five is five over one — flip it to one fifth.
  (does: Write "= 5/6 · 1/5 = 1/6")
  SAYS: Five sixths times one fifth: one sixth.
  SAYS: And you can never divide by zero — so zero has no reciprocal.
  --- slide 6: Stacked fractions
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 5/6 over 10/9 (a fraction of fractions)
  [board] $\dfrac{\frac{5}{6}}{\frac{10}{9}}$
  SAYS: Looks scary. It isn't.
  SAYS: Find the main fraction bar — the longest one, level with where the equals sign would sit.
  (does: Trace over the long main bar)
  SAYS: So it's five sixths DIVIDED BY ten ninths.
  (does: Write "= 5/6 : 10/9")
  SAYS: Rewrite it on one line. Now it's plain division.
  (does: Write "= 5/6 · 9/10")
  SAYS: Multiply by the reciprocal.
  (does: Cancel 5 with 10 (1 and 2) and 9 with 6 (3 and 2); write "= 3/4")
  SAYS: Five and ten: one and two. Nine and six: three and two. Three quarters.
  SAYS: There's an old trick with arcs — the "ears" method. Here it is, just so you've seen it.
  SAYS: Outer numbers on top: five times nine. Inner numbers underneath: six times ten.
  (does: Beside it write "(5·9)/(6·10) = 45/60 = 3/4")
  SAYS: Same answer. But people swap what goes on top all the time — so my advice: skip it.
  SAYS: Multiply by the reciprocal and you'll never get confused.
  --- slide 7: Whole over a fraction
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2 over 3/4
  [board] $\dfrac{2}{\frac{3}{4}}$
  SAYS: One more stacked one — with a whole number on top.
  SAYS: Which bar is the main one? The longer one — level with the equals sign.
  (does: Trace over the long main bar)
  SAYS: So it's two divided by three quarters.
  (does: Write "= 2/1 : 3/4")
  SAYS: Turn the two into a fraction: two over one.
  (does: Write "= 2/1 · 4/3 = 8/3")
  SAYS: Multiply by the reciprocal: two over one times four thirds. Eight thirds.
  (does: Write "= 2 2/3")
  SAYS: Three goes into eight twice, two left over — two and two thirds. If the answers show eight thirds, you're already done.
  --- slide 8: Mixed numbers
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 1 2/3 · 3/5
  [board] $1\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{5}$
  SAYS: Mixed numbers need one extra step: turn them into improper fractions first.
  (does: Write "= 5/3 · 3/5")
  SAYS: One and two thirds is five thirds.
  (does: Write "= 1")
  SAYS: Five thirds times three fifths… everything cancels. One! They're reciprocals.
  SAYS: DON'T multiply the whole parts and the fraction parts separately. A mixed number is a sum — that shortcut breaks it.
  --- slide 9: What division means
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 3/4 : 1/8
  [board] $\frac{3}{4}:\frac{1}{8}$
  SAYS: Why does dividing by a fraction make the answer BIGGER? Let's see what it means.
  SAYS: How many eighths fit into three quarters?
  SAYS: Three quarters is six eighths.
  (does: Number the shaded slices 1 to 6)
  SAYS: Six eighths fit. So the answer is six.
  (does: In the question, write "= 6")
  SAYS: Divide by a small fraction and the answer gets BIGGER.
  SAYS: That's not weird — you're counting how many little pieces fit.
  --- slide 10: Recap
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{c}{d}=\frac{ac}{bd}$
  [board] Cancel first — only across multiplication.
  [board] Divide $\to$ keep, change, flip the second.
  [board] Mixed numbers $\to$ improper fractions first.
  SAYS: Let's lock it in.
  (does: Circle "the second")
  SAYS: Five questions next. Cancel before you multiply — and know which factor you removed.
  SAYS: Next: adding and subtracting fractions. Different operation — different rule.

### Q None (q-071): \(\frac{6}{12} \cdot  \frac{2}{8} = ?\)
  (1) \(\frac{1}{6}\)
  (2) \(\frac{1}{8}\)
  (3) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
  (4) \(\frac{1}{3}\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Reduce each factor first: 6:12 = 1:2 and 2:8 = 1:4 . Then 1:2 · 1:4 = 1:8 .

### Q None (q-072): \(\frac{15}{14} \cdot  4\,\frac{2}{3} = ?\)
  (1) \(7\)
  (2) \(5\)
  (3) \(4\)
  (4) \(6\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Convert: 4 2:3 = 14:3 . Then 15:14 · 14:3 — the fourteens cancel — leaving 15:3 = 5 .

### Q None (q-073): \(\frac{7}{12} :  \frac{1}{3} = ?\)
  (1) \(1\,\frac{3}{4}\)
  (2) \(\frac{7}{36}\)
  (3) \(\frac{4}{7}\)
  (4) \(1\,\frac{1}{4}\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Flip and multiply: 7:12 · 3:1 = 21:12 = 7:4 = 1 3:4 . The distractor 7:36 comes from multiplying by 1:3 without flipping.

### Q None (q-074): \(\frac{\frac{7}{5}}{\frac{3}{5}} = ?\)

  (3) \(2\,\frac{2}{3}\)
  (4) \(1\,\frac{2}{3}\)
  correct: 0

### Q None (q-075): \(8 :  3\,\frac{1}{5} = ?\)

## Section: 3 · Adding and subtracting (learn)

### VIDEO: Adding & Subtracting Fractions (3.7 min)
  --- slide 1: Adding & Subtracting Fractions
  [on screen] Massive title — "Adding & Subtracting Fractions"
  SAYS: Adding and subtracting fractions.
  SAYS: Still the foundations — so this has to become automatic.
  SAYS: And watch out: the rule here is DIFFERENT from multiplying.
  --- slide 2: Same denominator
  [on screen] Blank
  SAYS: Two sevenths.
  SAYS: Plus three sevenths.
  SAYS: Put them together: five sevenths.
  (does: Beside the bars write "2/7 + 3/7 = 5/7")
  SAYS: The pieces are all sevenths. We add HOW MANY pieces — the numerators.
  SAYS: The denominator stays seven. The pieces didn't change size.
  SAYS: Two apples plus three apples is five apples.
  SAYS: Subtraction works the same way: six sevenths minus two sevenths — four sevenths.
  SAYS: But what if one bar were cut into eighths and the other into sixths? The pieces don't line up — you can't just add them.
  SAYS: For that, you need a common denominator.
  --- slide 3: Expand just one
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 3/10 + 1/5
  [board] $\frac{3}{10}+\frac{1}{5}$
  SAYS: Tenths and fifths. Different denominators — so we make them match.
  SAYS: Can five reach ten? Yes — times two. So only ONE fraction has to change.
  (does: Write "×2" on top and bottom of 1/5, then "= 2/10")
  SAYS: One fifth becomes two tenths. Top AND bottom — times two. That keeps its value.
  (does: Write "= 3/10 + 2/10 = 5/10")
  SAYS: Three tenths stays exactly as it is. Three plus two: five tenths.
  (does: Write "= 1/2")
  SAYS: And five tenths reduces to one half.
  --- slide 4: Expand both
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 1/2 + 1/3
  [board] $\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}$
  SAYS: Halves and thirds. Different-sized pieces.
  SAYS: You can't just count "two pieces" — they're different sizes.
  SAYS: Can two become three? No. So BOTH fractions change.
  SAYS: Stuck finding a denominator? Just multiply them: two times three — six. Cut both pizzas into sixths.
  (does: Under the new pies write "3/6" and "2/6")
  SAYS: The half becomes three sixths. The third becomes two sixths.
  (does: On 1/2 write "×3" on top and bottom; on 1/3 write "×2" on top and bottom)
  SAYS: Times three — top AND bottom. Times two — top and bottom. Change only the bottoms and you've changed the amounts.
  (does: In the question, write "= 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6")
  SAYS: Now we can count: five sixths.
  SAYS: That's all a common denominator is — naming both amounts in the same-sized piece.
  --- slide 5: Common denominator
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 3/8 + 5/12
  [board] $\frac{3}{8}+\frac{5}{12}$
  SAYS: Eight and twelve. We could multiply them: ninety-six. That works — but it's big.
  SAYS: Faster: take the BIGGER denominator and run through its multiples.
  (does: Beside the question write "12 ✗  24 ✓")
  SAYS: Twelve — does eight go in? No. Twenty-four — yes. Both eight and twelve fit.
  (does: Write "×3" on top and bottom of 3/8, and "×2" on top and bottom of 5/12)
  SAYS: Eight needs times three. Twelve needs times two. Top and bottom.
  (does: Write "= 9/24 + 10/24 = 19/24")
  SAYS: Nine twenty-fourths plus ten twenty-fourths: nineteen twenty-fourths.
  SAYS: Used ninety-six anyway? Totally fine — you'd just reduce at the end.
  SAYS: Don't spend longer hunting for the denominator than it takes to calculate.
  --- slide 6: Subtracting
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 5/6 − 1/4
  [board] $\frac{5}{6}-\frac{1}{4}$
  SAYS: Subtraction — exactly the same steps.
  SAYS: Common denominator of six and four. Multiples of six: six — no. Twelve — yes.
  (does: Write "= 10/12 − 3/12")
  SAYS: Six times two: ten twelfths. Four times three: three twelfths.
  (does: Write "= 7/12")
  SAYS: Ten minus three: seven twelfths.
  SAYS: Tidy habit: write the sign between the expanded fractions before you combine them. It kills sign errors.
  --- slide 7: Whole & mixed numbers
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2 − 3/5
  [board] $2-\frac{3}{5}$
  [board] $3\frac{1}{6}-1\frac{3}{4}$
  SAYS: A whole number can wear any denominator you like.
  (does: Write "= 10/5 − 3/5 = 7/5")
  SAYS: Two is ten fifths. Minus three fifths: seven fifths.
  SAYS: Mixed numbers? Turn them into improper fractions first. No special borrowing rules needed.
  (does: Write "= 19/6 − 7/4")
  SAYS: Three and one sixth: nineteen sixths. One and three quarters: seven quarters.
  (does: Write "= 38/12 − 21/12 = 17/12")
  SAYS: In twelfths: thirty-eight minus twenty-one — seventeen twelfths.
  (does: Write "= 1 5/12")
  SAYS: One and five twelfths.
  --- slide 8: Recap
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $+\ \ -$ $\to$ common denominator, then add the tops
  [board] $\times$ $\to$ top times top, bottom times bottom
  [board] $:$ $\to$ multiply by the reciprocal
  SAYS: All four operations, side by side.
  (does: Circle the + − line)
  SAYS: Most fraction mistakes come from using the right rule in the wrong place.
  SAYS: So before you calculate, name the operation. Then say the rule.
  SAYS: Five questions next. Then: decimal fractions.

### Q None (q-067): \(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{8} = ?\)
  (1) \(\frac{1}{8}\)

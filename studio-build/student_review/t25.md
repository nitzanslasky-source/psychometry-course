  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $4,\quad 6,\quad 8$
  [board] $4,\quad 6,\quad 6,\quad 8$

  SAYS: Four, six, eight. The average is six — and notice, six is also one of the numbers.
  (does: Write "avg 6" under the list)
  SAYS: Now add another six. What's the average?
  (does: Write "avg 6" under the new list)
  SAYS: Still six. A number equal to the average sits right on the middle — not left, not right.
  SAYS: That's our first rule: add or remove numbers equal to the average, and the average doesn't move.
  --- slide 4: Towers of blocks
  [on screen] Blank
  SAYS: Now the harder case — a group that isn't symmetric.
  SAYS: Five towers of blocks: three, eight, four, ten, five.
  SAYS: Before we calculate — let's box the average in.
  (does: Circle the tallest tower (10) and the shortest (3))
  SAYS: If we move blocks from tall towers to short ones, no tower can end up taller than ten, or shorter than three.

  SAYS: Six is the average. We didn't add or remove a single block — we just shared them equally.
  --- slide 5: The formula
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\text{average}=\dfrac{\text{sum of the values}}{\text{number of values}}$
  [board] $\dfrac{3+8+4+10+5}{5}$
  SAYS: So what did we actually do?
  SAYS: We took all the blocks — the sum — and shared them among the towers — the number of values.
  (does: Write "= 30/5 = 6")

  SAYS: That's the formula you know from school — and now you know why it works.
  --- slide 6: One value changes
  [on screen] Blank

  SAYS: A quick extra. Five scores, and one of them goes up by ten.
  SAYS: The total goes up by ten. Shared over five scores —

  SAYS: — the average goes up by two. We never needed the original scores.
  --- slide 7: Recap
  [on screen] Blank

  [board] Adding the average doesn't change it.

### Q 1 (wp25-g082): Nine students solve an average of 8 puzzles each in one afternoon. How many puzzles do they solve altogether?
  (1) 80
  (2) 81
  (3) 72
  (4) 64
  correct: 2
  solution: Average means total divided by count. Reversing that relationship, the total is count times average: nine times eight equals seventy-two. The students need not each solve exactly eight; the average tells us their combined total.

### Q 3 (wp25-g084): Four numbers are given. Take the average of each of the six different pairs, then add those six averages. The result equals which of the following?
  (1) 4 times their average
  (2) 6 times the average of the four numbers
  (3) 2 times their average
  (4) 3 times their average
  correct: 1
  solution: Each of the four numbers appears in three of the six pairs. Each appearance contributes half of that number to a pair average. The sum of pair averages is so \(\frac32(a+b+c+d)\).
  solution: The overall average is \(\frac{a+b+c+d}{4}\). Multiplying that by six also gives \(\frac32(a+b+c+d)\). So the required total is six times the overall average.

### Q 4 (wp25-g085): Kai and Zara take two tests, art and science. Zara’s art score is 5 points higher than Kai’s. Kai’s two-test average is 3 points higher than Zara’s. How much higher is Kai’s science score than Zara’s?
  (1) 6
  (2) 8
  (3) 16
  (4) 11
  correct: 3
  solution: Across two equally weighted tests, an average lead of three means a total-score lead of six. Kai is five behind in art. To finish six ahead overall, Kai must make up those five and gain six more in science: an eleven-point lead.

  --- slide 1: Weighted Averages
  [on screen] Massive title — "Weighted Averages"

  SAYS: So far the average sat exactly in the middle. Now some values count more than others.
  --- slide 2: Different weights
  [on screen] Blank

  SAYS: A weighted average is an average where the values have different importance — different weights.
  SAYS: You know it from your matriculation grades: a five-unit exam counts five times as much as a one-unit exam.
  SAYS: The heavier value pulls the average toward itself.
  SAYS: And watch out: on the exam it'll just say "average" — you have to notice it's weighted.
  --- slide 3: Repeated copies
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Score 70 with weight 3; score 90 with weight 1
  SAYS: Seventy with weight three, ninety with weight one.
  SAYS: Weight three means: treat it as if it appeared three times.
  (does: Write "70 + 70 + 70 + 90")
  SAYS: Seventy, seventy, seventy — and ninety.
  (does: Write "= 300 ÷ 4 = 75")
  SAYS: Three hundred. And divide by four — not by two. We now have four copies.
  SAYS: Seventy-five: closer to seventy, because seventy weighs more.
  --- slide 4: The formula
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\text{weighted average}=\dfrac{\text{value}_1\cdot\text{weight}_1+\text{value}_2\cdot\text{weight}_2}{\text{weight}_1+\text{weight}_2}$
  SAYS: That's exactly the weighted-average formula.
  SAYS: Each value times its weight — and divide by the sum of the weights.
  (does: Under it write "(70·3 + 90·1)/(3 + 1) = 75")
  SAYS: More values? Each one times its own weight. Same idea.
  --- slide 5: The see-saw
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Weights $3:1\ \to$ distances $1:3$
  SAYS: Now the psychometric picture: the average is a balance point — a see-saw.
  SAYS: The heavy side pulls the pivot toward it. The average moves closer to the bigger weight.
  (does: Mark the distances: 5 (from 70) and 15 (to 90))
  SAYS: Seventy-five is five from seventy, fifteen from ninety.
  (does: Write "3 × 5 = 1 × 15")
  SAYS: Three weights times five — equals one weight times fifteen. Balanced.
  SAYS: The distances are the weights — flipped. Three times the weight means three times closer.
  --- slide 6: Split the gap
  [on screen] Blank
  SAYS: So you can find the average without the formula.
  SAYS: The gap is twenty. The distances must be one to three — four parts.
  (does: Split the gap into 4 parts of 5; mark the mean one part above 70)
  SAYS: Twenty over four: parts of five. One part from seventy: seventy-five.
  SAYS: On the exam, draw exactly this: an axis, the two values above, the weights below.
  --- slide 7: Groups as weights
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] 6 values average 50; 9 values average 70
  SAYS: When you combine groups, the size of each group is its weight.
  (does: Write "6 : 9 = 2 : 3")
  SAYS: Six to nine is two to three. Reduce the weights first — it doesn't change the average.
  (does: Write "(2·50 + 3·70)/5 = 310/5 = 62")

  --- slide 8: Recap
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Weight = number of copies

### Q 5 (wp25-g087): A course grade gives an exam twice the weight of a project. A student scores 68 on the exam and 92 on the project. What is the course grade?
  (1) 76
  (2) 74
  (3) 80
  (4) 84
  correct: 0
  solution: The two exam weight units act like two copies of sixty-eight; the project adds one copy of ninety-two. Add 68+68+92=228 and divide by the three total weight units, not by the two assessment names. The result is seventy-six.
  solution: This builds the weighted-average formula from ordinary averaging. A larger weight means that value contributes more copies to both the numerator and the total count of weight units.

### Q 8 (wp25-g090): A booklet has 1,200 lines. Every line contains either 4 or 6 words, and the average is 4.5 words per line. How many lines contain 6 words?
  (1) 300
  (2) 200
  (3) 400
  (4) 600
  correct: 0
  solution: If every line had four words, the booklet would have 4,800 words. The actual average gives 1,200×4.5=5,400. The extra six hundred words come in additions of two for each six-word line. Six hundred divided by two gives three hundred such lines.

  (2) The teams have equal sizes
  (3) The sizes cannot be compared
  (4) Team A has more members
  correct: 3
  solution: Seventy is six above Team A’s average and twelve below Team B’s. The result sits closer to A, so A has more weight. In fact, A needs twice as many members to balance the twice-as-large distance on B’s side.

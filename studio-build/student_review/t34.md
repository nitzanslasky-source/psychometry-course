  [on screen] Blank

  SAYS: Every triangle has 180 inside it. So just add them up.

  SAYS: Remember — that's exactly what we did in the quadrilateral: one diagonal, two triangles, 180 plus 180, 360.
  SAYS: 7 sides, 5 triangles. Always n minus 2 — that's where the formula comes from.
  --- slide 5: +1 side = +180°
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v486.png - Regular 5-sided polygon]
  [board] $5: 540°\ \rightarrow\ 6: 720°\ \rightarrow\ 7: 900°\ \rightarrow\ 8: 1080°$

  (does: Draw a small triangle on side CD, then cross out CD)

  SAYS: What really happened? I added one more triangle.

  SAYS: Pentagon 540. Hexagon 720. Heptagon 900. Octagon 1080. Every extra side — plus 180.
  --- slide 6: Regular
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v487.png - ]
  [board] Regular $=$ all sides equal AND all angles equal
  SAYS: A regular polygon is a polygon where ALL the sides and ALL the angles are equal.
  SAYS: For example, a regular hexagon: 6 sides — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — all the sides equal, and all the angles equal.
  SAYS: Why do we stress that the angles are equal too? Because of this.
  SAYS: I can take two sides and fold them inward — and I get a hexagon where all the sides are still equal.
  SAYS: These two sides are exactly those two sides. But it's no longer a regular hexagon.
  SAYS: A regular polygon is symmetric — it looks the same from every direction.
  --- slide 7: On a circle
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v488.png - Regular 5-sided polygon]
  [board] Regular $\Rightarrow$ fits in a circle
  [board] Fits in a circle $\not\Rightarrow$ regular
  SAYS: Every regular polygon can be inscribed in a circle.
  SAYS: We draw a circle around the regular polygon — and all its vertices sit on the circumference.
  (does: Mark the centre O)
  SAYS: The point in the middle is the centre of the circle.
  SAYS: By the way, a circle can also be drawn inside every regular polygon — less interesting for us right now.
  SAYS: Careful — it only works one way. If a polygon fits in a circle, that does NOT mean it's regular.
  SAYS: I could move this vertex along the circle and stretch a side — still on the circle, not regular.
  SAYS: And the folded hexagon from before? It can't be inscribed in a circle — so it's definitely not regular.
  --- slide 8: One angle
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] One angle $=\frac{180°\cdot(n-2)}{n}$
  [board] Pentagon: $540°:5=108°$
  SAYS: The size of one angle in a regular polygon.
  SAYS: The angle sum we already know how to find: 180 times n minus 2.
  SAYS: But I want just one angle.
  SAYS: The polygon is regular — all the angles are equal. So I just divide by the number of angles.
  SAYS: n sides — n angles. So: the angle sum, divided by n.
  SAYS: Pentagon: 540 divided by 5 — 108.
  --- slide 9: The formula sheet
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $\frac{180°\cdot(n-2)}{n}=\frac{180n-360}{n}$
  [board] $=\frac{180n}{n}-\frac{360}{n}=180°-\frac{360°}{n}$
  SAYS: Now let's open the brackets.
  SAYS: 180n minus 360, over n.
  SAYS: Now split the fraction — the opposite of a common denominator.
  SAYS: 180n over n — the n cancels. We get 180 minus 360 over n.
  SAYS: Why am I showing you this? Because this is the formula on the psychometric formula sheet. Know it when you see it.
  SAYS: Most students actually remember the first one — it's more intuitive: the angle sum divided by the number of angles.
  SAYS: It's exactly the same formula. Just simplified.
  --- slide 10: Three to know
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Tip: central angle $\frac{360°}{n}$ $\rightarrow$ complete to $180°$
  SAYS: Here's a table of the three regular polygons that show up most on the exam.
  SAYS: Pentagon, hexagon, octagon. 5, 6 and 8 sides. These are angles worth remembering.
  SAYS: Angle sums: 540, 720, 1080.
  SAYS: The central angle is the angle in the middle — at the centre of the circle, when we draw radii to the vertices.
  SAYS: In a hexagon, 6 radii give 6 angles — one opposite each side. 360 over 6 — 60.
  SAYS: 360 over 8 — 45. 360 over 5 — 72. Just like arcs and sectors: a fifth, a sixth, an eighth of a circle.
  SAYS: The angle itself is sometimes hard to remember. The hexagon — almost everyone remembers: 60 and 120.
  SAYS: Pentagon and octagon — less. So here's a tip. The central angle is easy: octagon, 360 over 8 is 45 — half of 90.
  (does: Write 45° + 135° = 180°)
  SAYS: The polygon's angle simply completes it to 180. 45 plus what is 180? 135.
  (does: Write 72° + 108° = 180°)
  SAYS: 72 — to get to 180 I need another 108. And 60 plus 120.
  SAYS: Don't remember the angle? Fine. Find the central angle in seconds and complete it to 180.
  --- slide 11: Diagonals: equal parts
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v489.png - Regular 5-sided polygon]
  [board] Equal arcs $\rightarrow$ equal inscribed angles
  [board] $108°:3=36°$
  SAYS: In a regular polygon, the diagonals divide the polygon's angle into equal angles.
  SAYS: From this vertex I drew two diagonals. In a pentagon, that's all I can draw — more sides, more diagonals.
  (does: Mark the three angles at A as equal)
  SAYS: The diagonals split the pentagon's angle into 3 angles — and they're equal.
  SAYS: Why? As we learned about 2 minutes ago — every regular polygon can be inscribed in a circle.
  SAYS: Look at this angle: its vertex is on the circle — it's an inscribed angle. And it rests on this arc.
  SAYS: The polygon is regular — so all the arcs are equal.
  SAYS: Inscribed angles on equal arcs are equal. So the diagonals cut the angle into equal angles.
  SAYS: Here: 108 divided by 3 — 36 each.
  --- slide 12: Isosceles triangles
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v490.png - Regular 8-sided polygon]
  [board] $n$ sides $\rightarrow$ $n$ congruent isosceles triangles
  SAYS: Every regular polygon is made of n congruent isosceles triangles.
  SAYS: For example, a regular octagon. I draw all the radii from the centre of the circle around it.
  (does: Mark two radii of one triangle as equal)
  SAYS: Here's an isosceles triangle: this side equals this side — they're both radii of the circle.
  SAYS: Why n triangles? Because there are n sides. 8 sides — 8 triangles.
  --- slide 13: The hexagon
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] [FIGURE: fig/v491.png - Regular 6-sided polygon]
  [board] $360°:6=60°$ $\rightarrow$ 6 equilateral triangles
  SAYS: The exception is the hexagon.
  SAYS: A regular hexagon is made of 6 EQUILATERAL triangles.
  SAYS: Actually it's not really an exception — it's just more regular than the other regular ones.
  SAYS: It also splits into isosceles triangles — radius and radius.
  (does: Write 60° at the centre in one triangle)
  SAYS: But in the hexagon, the head angle is 360 over 6 — 60 degrees. So the triangle is equilateral.
  SAYS: Very important to know this split of the hexagon into equilateral triangles.
  SAYS: We'll use it for areas — and for angles — later on.
  --- slide 14: Recap
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Angle sum: $180°(n-2)$ — or split into triangles
  [board] Regular: one angle $=\frac{180°(n-2)}{n}=180°-\frac{360°}{n}$
  [board] Diagonals from one vertex $\rightarrow$ equal angles (inscribed angles on equal arcs)
  [board] Radii $\rightarrow$ $n$ congruent isosceles triangles · hexagon: 6 equilateral
  SAYS: That's polygons — there really aren't many rules. Let's review.
  SAYS: Angle sum: remember 180 times n minus 2 — or just split into triangles. A pentagon: 1, 2, 3 triangles, 3 times 180.
  SAYS: The diagonals split the angle into equal angles — because they're inscribed angles in the circle.
  SAYS: And every regular polygon splits into isosceles triangles — again because of the circle: they're radii.
  SAYS: The regular hexagon is the special case: equilateral triangles, not just isosceles.
  SAYS: That's polygons. Let's move on.

  SAYS: So how do we find the area of a polygon?
  SAYS: We break it into pieces — shapes we DO know.
  SAYS: Once we know the area of each piece, we know the area of the whole polygon.
  SAYS: A diagonal, a height — whatever gives you a base and a matching height.
  SAYS: And sometimes it's easier the other way: surround it with a big shape and subtract what's missing.
  SAYS: Let's see some examples.

### Q 1 (geo34-g106): In pentagon ABCDE, angles B, C and E are right angles. Given: \(AB=BC=AE=12\) cm and \(DE=5\) cm, with AB parallel to CD. What is the area of the pentagon (in cm²)?
  [FIGURE: fig/geo34-g106.png - An irregular pentagon split into a square and two right triangles]
  (1) \(180\)
  (2) \(234\)
  (3) \(204\)
  (4) \(174\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Triangle AED has perpendicular legs 12 and 5, so its area is 30 and AD=13. Drop AH perpendicular to CD. ABCH is a square of side 12. Triangle ADH has hypotenuse 13 and height 12, so DH=5 and its area is 30. The total is 144+30+30=204.

### Q 3 (geo34-g108): A regular octagon has side length 5 cm. What is its area (in cm²)?
  [FIGURE: fig/geo34-g108.png - Regular octagon]
  (1) \(100\)
  (2) \(50+50\sqrt2\)
  (3) \(25+25\sqrt2\)
  (4) \(50+25\sqrt2\)
  correct: 1
  solution: The central square has area 25. Each corner right isosceles triangle has hypotenuse 5, so its legs are 5:√2 and its area 25:4. The four triangles total 25. Each rectangle has sides 5 and 5:√2 and area 25√2:2; four total 50√2. Altogether the area is 50+50√2.

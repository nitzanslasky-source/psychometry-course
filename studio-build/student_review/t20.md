### Q 1 (q-577): Given: a, b and c are three integers, \(c < b < a\), and \(a + b < 12\). Which of the following numbers cannot be the value of (\(b + c\))?
  (1) \(1\)
  (2) \(-1\)
  (3) \(10\)
  (4) \(-10\)
  correct: 2
  solution: Suppose b + c = 10 . Since c < b , we'd need 2b > b + c = 10 , so b ≥ 6 (integers). Then a > b forces a ≥ 7 , giving a + b ≥ 13 — contradicting a + b < 12 . So b + c = 10 is impossible. The other values are achievable: b+c = 1 with (a,b,c) = (2,1,0) ; b+c = −1 with (1,0, −1 ); b+c = −10 with (0, −4 , −6 ) — all satisfy c < b < a and a+b < 12 .

### Q 2 (q-578): Given: \(0 < a\) and \(0 < x\), and \({x}^{2} = {a}^{3}\). If a is increased by a factor of 9, then x increases by a factor of —
  (1) \(36\)
  (2) \(18\)
  (3) \(27\)
  (4) \(9\)
  correct: 2
  solution: The new value a' = 9a gives x'² = (9a)³ = 729·a³ = 729·x² . Taking the positive root (x > 0) : x' = √729 · x = 27x . So x grows by a factor of 27.

### Q 3 (q-579): Given: a, b and c are three integers, distinct and positive, and \(\frac{{c}^{2}}{ab} = \frac{a}{b} + \frac{b}{a}\). Which of the following statements is not necessarily true?
  (1) \(c < a + b\)
  (2) \(c < 6\)
  (3) \(b < c\)
  (4) \(a < c\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Multiply both sides by ab: c² = a² + b² — a Pythagorean-type relation. (1) is always true: (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b² > a² + b² = c² (since ab > 0 ), so c < a+b . (3) is always true: c² = a² + b² > b² , so c > b . (4) is always true by the same argument with a. But (2) is not necessary: the Pythagorean triple (a,b,c) = (5,12,13) satisfies the given — 13² = 169 = 25 + 144 — and there c = 13 , which is not below 6.

### Q 4 (q-580): A plant's growth rate is determined by a formula: it increases the more sunlight (S) the plant receives and the more water (W) it gets, and decreases the greater the amount of weeds (D) around it \(\left(1 < S,\;W,\;D\right)\). Which of the following expressions cannot express the plant's growth rate?
  (1) \(\frac{{S}^{W}}{D}\)
  (2) \(S \cdot  W - D\)
  (3) \(S - D + W\)
  (4) \(S \cdot  W \cdot  D\)
  correct: 3
  solution: A valid formula must increase in S, increase in W, and decrease in D. (1) Sᵂ:D : grows with S and W (base and exponent above 1), and dividing by D makes it fall as D grows ✓. (2) S·W−D : the product grows with S and W, and subtracting D makes it fall as D grows ✓. (3) S−D+W : rises with S and W, falls with D ✓. (4) S·W·D : rises with S and W — but also rises as D grows, since D multiplies the expression. That violates the requirement that growth decreases with more weeds, so (4) cannot express the growth rate.

### Q None (q-581): Given: a, b and c are three integers, \(0 < c < b < a\). Which of the following possibilities is necessarily not correct?
  (1) \(b = 3\) and \(a = 4\)
  (2) \(c = 2\) and \(b = 3\)
  (3) \(c = 2\) and \(b = 3\) and \(a = 6\)
  (4) \(b = 1\) and \(a = 3\)
  correct: 3
  solution: (1) works with c = 1 or 2: e.g. 0 < 2 < 3 < 4 ✓. (2) works with any a ≥ 4 : 0 < 2 < 3 < 4 ✓. (3) is fully specified and valid: 0 < 2 < 3 < 6 ✓. (4) is impossible: with b = 1 , we'd need an integer c with 0 < c < 1 , and no integer lies strictly between 0 and 1.

### Q None (q-582): Given: a, b and c are three different even numbers with \(a < b < c\) and \(c - a < 8\). Let \(x = b - a\). x can be —
  (1) \(1\)
  (2) \(2\)
  (3) \(6\)
  (4) \(8\)
  correct: 1
  solution: x = b − a is a difference of two even numbers, so x is even — ruling out 1. Also c ≥ b + 2 (both even, distinct), so c − a ≥ x + 2 ; requiring c − a < 8 gives x + 2 ≤ c−a ≤ 6 , so x ≤ 4 — ruling out 6 and 8. x = 2 is achievable: (a,b,c) = (2,4,6) gives c − a = 4 < 8 ✓.

### Q None (q-583): Nadia chose 13 positive integers, all different from each other. Within the group of numbers she chose, there necessarily exist two numbers whose difference is divisible by —
  (1) \(12\)
  (2) \(13\)
  (3) \(14\)
  (4) \(15\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Pigeonhole principle: there are only 12 possible remainders upon division by 12 (0 through 11). With 13 numbers, at least two must share the same remainder mod 12, and their difference is then divisible by 12. This is not guaranteed for 13, 14 or 15: thirteen numbers can occupy thirteen distinct remainders in those larger moduli (e.g. the numbers 1..13 have all-different remainders mod 14), leaving no pair with a difference divisible by them.

### Q None (q-584): a, b and c are positive integers, distinct, with \(a < b < c\). Given: \(a + b + c = 12\). What is the smallest value the expression (\(c - a\)) can take?
  (1) \(1\)
  (2) \(2\)
  (3) \(3\)
  (4) \(4\)
  correct: 1
  solution: c − a = 1 is impossible: b would have to be an integer strictly between a and a+1 , and none exists. c − a = 2 forces the triple to be consecutive, (a, a+1 , a+2 ), with sum 3a + 3 = 12 , giving a = 3 — the triple (3, 4, 5), which is valid: distinct, increasing, sum 12 ✓. So the minimum of c − a is 2.

### Q None (q-585): x, y and z are positive integers, distinct from each other. Which of the following statements cannot be correct regarding the three numbers x:y , y:z , z:x ?
  (1) only one of them is smaller than \(1\)
  (2) only two of them are smaller than \(1\)
  (3) the sum of all three together is greater than \(1\)
  (4) each one of the three is greater than \(1\)
  correct: 3
  solution: The product (x:y)·(y:z)·(z:x) = 1 always. If all three fractions were greater than 1, their product would exceed 1 — contradiction. So (4) is impossible, so certainly not necessarily correct. (Claims (1) and (2) describe situations that can each occur for suitable x, y, z, and (3) is in fact always true: three positive numbers with product 1 that are not all equal have a sum greater than 3, which is greater than 1.)

### Q None (q-586): Given: x is an odd number, y is an even number, \(x < y\). What is the number of odd numbers between x and y (not including x)?
  (1) \(\frac{y - x}{2}\)
  (2) \(\frac{y - x}{2} - 1\)
  (3) \(\frac{y - x + 1}{2} + 1\)
  (4) \(\frac{y - x - 1}{2}\)
  correct: 3
  solution: The odd numbers strictly after x and up to y are x+2 , x+4 , …, y−1 (y itself is even). Their count is ((y−1) − (x+2)):2 + 1 = (y−x−1):2 . Verify with x=1 , y=6 : odds 3, 5 — two of them; formula (6−1−1):2 = 2 ✓. With x=1 , y=8 : odds 3, 5, 7 — three; formula (8−1−1):2 = 3 ✓.

### Q None (q-587): a, b and c are three different integers. Given: \(a + b + c = 25\). Which of the following statements is necessarily true?
  (1) b is not equal to the average of a and c
  (2) the smallest number among a, b, c is \(\text{odd}\)
  (3) c is not equal to \(5\)
  (4) the sum of any two of the numbers is greater than the third
  correct: 0
  solution: If b were the average of a and c, then a + c = 2b , so a + b + c = 3b = 25 — but 25 is not divisible by 3, so no whole b exists. Hence b can never equal that average — always true. Counterexamples sink the others: (2) fails with (2, 3, 20) — smallest is 2, even; (3) fails with (3, 17, 5) — c = 5 ; (4) fails with (1, 2, 22) — 1 + 2 = 3 < 22 .

### Q None (q-588): Given: a and x are positive integers, and \(a < x < 3\,a\). For a given fixed value of a, how many different values can x take?
  (1) \(2\,a - 1\)
  (2) \(2\,a\)
  (3) \(2\,a + 1\)
  (4) \(a - 1\)
  correct: 0
  solution: x runs over the integers strictly between a and 3a : from a+1 up to 3a−1 . Their count is (3a−1) − (a+1) + 1 = 2a − 1 . Verify with a = 2 : x ∈ {3, 4, 5}, three values, and 2·2 − 1 = 3 ✓. With a = 3 : x ∈ {4,…,8}, five values = 2·3 − 1 ✓.

### Q None (q-589): Given: a is a positive number, and y = aᵃ . If a is increased by a factor of 3, then y is multiplied by a factor of —
  (1) \({a}^{a}\)
  (2) \({a}^{3}\)
  (3) \({3}^{a} \cdot  {a}^{a}\)
  (4) \({3}^{3\,a} \cdot  {a}^{2\,a}\)
  correct: 3
  solution: The new value is y' = (3a)^(3a) = 3^(3a) · a^(3a) . The growth factor is y':y = 3^(3a) · a^(3a) : aᵃ = 3^(3a) · a^(2a) . Check with a = 1 : y = 1 , y' = 3³ = 27 , and the formula gives 3³·1² = 27 ✓.

### Q None (q-590): On a board are written 13 different positive integers, each of which begins with the digit 5 and ends with the digit 2. Which of the following possibilities is not achievable?
  (1) all the numbers are composed of only the digits \(5\) and \(2\)
  (2) all the numbers have \(3\) digits or fewer
  (3) all the numbers have \(5\) digits or more
  (4) all the numbers are divisible by \(9\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Numbers that start with 5 and end with 2 with at most 3 digits: the two-digit "52", plus the ten three-digit numbers " 5X2 " for X = 0 ..9 — a total of only 11 candidates. Thirteen different numbers cannot be drawn from a pool of 11, so (2) is impossible. The others are achievable: (1) using only digits 5 and 2 there are plenty (52, 552, 522, 5552, 5522, 5252, 5222, 55552, …); (3) five-digit-and-up candidates are unlimited; (4) multiples of 9 of this shape are also unlimited (e.g. 522 = 9·58 , 5022, 5112, … — pad with digits to reach digit sums divisible by 9).

### Q None (alg-extra-unit-t20-2-3): If \(x^2+y^2=0\) for real x,y, which of the following is necessarily true?

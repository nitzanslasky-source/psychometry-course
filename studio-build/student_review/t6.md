  --- slide 2: Isolate x
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 4x + 7 = 31
  [board] $4x+7=31$
  SAYS: Two sides, and an equals sign in the middle. Whatever's on the left has the same value as whatever's on the right.
  SAYS: Our job: get x alone on one side.
  SAYS: The golden rule — whatever you do to one side, you do to the other. That keeps the balance.
  SAYS: The plus seven is in the way. So subtract seven — from BOTH sides.
  (does: Under it write "−7" on both sides, then "4x = 24")

  SAYS: But we want ONE x, not four. So divide both sides by four.

  SAYS: x equals six.
  (does: To the side write "4 · 6 + 7 = 31 ✓")
  SAYS: Check it in the original: four times six is twenty-four, plus seven — thirty-one. It works.
  SAYS: "Move it across and flip the sign" is just shorthand for doing the same thing to both sides.
  SAYS: And x doesn't have to end up on the left. Put it wherever it comes out positive — that's usually easier.
  --- slide 3: Every x works
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6
  [board] $2(x+3)=2x+6$
  SAYS: Not every equation gives you one neat answer.
  (does: Open the bracket: write "2x + 6 = 2x + 6")
  SAYS: Open the bracket: two x plus six. And the right side? Two x plus six. The exact same thing.
  (does: Cross out 2x on both sides; write "6 = 6")
  SAYS: Take two x off both sides: six equals six. Always true.
  SAYS: x disappeared. It doesn't matter what you plug in — the two sides are always equal.
  (does: Write "infinitely many solutions")
  SAYS: That's called infinitely many solutions. x can be any number.
  --- slide 4: No solution
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2(x + 3) = 2x + 9
  [board] $2(x+3)=2x+9$
  SAYS: Now the opposite situation.
  (does: Open the bracket: write "2x + 6 = 2x + 9")
  SAYS: Open the bracket: two x plus six equals two x plus nine.
  (does: Cross out 2x on both sides; write "6 = 9" and a big ✗)
  SAYS: Take two x off both sides: six equals nine.
  SAYS: We know that's false. That's a contradiction.
  SAYS: No matter which x you try, you'll hit that contradiction. So this equation has NO solution.
  --- slide 5: x in the denominator
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — (x + 2)/(x − 3) = 2
  [board] $\frac{x+2}{x-3}=2$
  SAYS: Equations with fractions. The method: a common denominator on both sides — then cancel it.
  SAYS: But when x is in the bottom, write the restriction FIRST.
  (does: Next to it write "x ≠ 3")
  SAYS: If x were three, the bottom would be zero. Not allowed. So x can't be three.
  (does: Rewrite the right side: "2 = 2(x − 3)/(x − 3)")
  SAYS: Give the right side the same denominator: two is two times x minus three, over x minus three.
  (does: Cross out both denominators; write "x + 2 = 2(x − 3)")
  SAYS: Same denominator on both sides of an equation — cancel it. Algebraically, we just multiplied both sides by x minus three.
  (does: Write "x + 2 = 2x − 6 → x = 8")
  SAYS: Open it: two x minus six. Collect: x equals eight.
  (does: Write "8 ≠ 3 ✓")
  SAYS: Eight isn't three — it's allowed. And ten over five is two. It checks.
  SAYS: If an answer ever makes a denominator zero — throw it out, even if it looks right.
  --- slide 6: Fractions & brackets
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 3(x − 2) + 5 = 2x + 8
  [board] $3(x-2)+5=2x+8$
  [board] $\frac{x+3}{4}=5$
  SAYS: Brackets first. The three multiplies EVERYTHING inside.
  (does: Write "3x − 6 + 5 = 2x + 8")
  SAYS: Three x minus six — not three x minus two. Then plus five.
  (does: Write "3x − 1 = 2x + 8")
  SAYS: Collect: three x minus one equals two x plus eight.
  (does: Write "x = 9")
  SAYS: x terms to one side, numbers to the other: x equals nine.
  SAYS: Now a plain number under the fraction. Same method: common denominator.
  (does: Write "5 = 20/4", then cross out both 4s: "x + 3 = 20")
  SAYS: Five is twenty quarters. Same denominator on both sides — cancel it. The four divided the WHOLE top, so we get x plus three equals twenty. Not x plus twelve!
  (does: Write "x = 17")
  SAYS: x equals seventeen.
  --- slide 7: Don't divide by x
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — x(x − 5) = 0
  [board] $x(x-5)=0$
  SAYS: Here's a trap that costs people points.
  SAYS: It's tempting to divide both sides by x. But what if x is zero? You can't divide by zero.
  (does: Next to it write "÷ x ✗")
  SAYS: Divide by x and you silently lose a solution.
  SAYS: Instead: a product equals zero when one of the factors is zero.
  (does: Write "x = 0  or  x − 5 = 0")
  SAYS: So either x is zero, or x minus five is zero.
  (does: Write "x = 0  or  x = 5")
  SAYS: Two solutions: zero and five. Keep them both.
  --- slide 8: Recap
  [on screen] Blank

  [board] $x$ cancels and it's true $\to$ infinitely many solutions
  [board] $x$ cancels and it's false $\to$ no solution
  [board] Never divide by something that could be $0$
  [board] $x$ in the denominator $\to$ write the restriction first
  SAYS: Let's lock it in.

### Q 1 (q-171): Given: \(x \ne  2\), \(x \ne  4\). \(\frac{3}{x - 4} = \frac{1}{x - 2}\). \(x = ?\)
  (1) \(1\)
  (2) \(31\)
  (3) \(21\)
  (4) \(2\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Cross-multiply: 3(x − 2) = 1·(x − 4) → 3x − 6 = x − 4 → 2x = 2 → x = 1 . Since 1 is not a forbidden value, it's the solution.

### Q None (q-167): \(4\,\left(3 - x\right) = 3\,\left(x - 3\right)\), \(x = ?\)
  (1) \(0\)
  (2) \(3\)
  (3) \(2\)
  (4) \(1\)
  correct: 1
  solution: Expand both sides: 12 − 4x = 3x − 9 . Move terms: 12 + 9 = 3x + 4x → 21 = 7x → x = 3 .

### Q None (q-168): \(\frac{4\,\left(x - 3\right)}{5} = 4\), \(x = ?\)
  (1) \(8\)
  (2) \(3\)
  (3) \(2\)
  (4) \(5\)
  correct: 0
  solution: Multiply both sides by 5: 4(x − 3) = 20 . Divide by 4: x − 3 = 5 , so x = 8 .

### Q None (q-169): \(\frac{8\,x + 12}{4} = \frac{6\,x + 9}{3}\), \(x = ?\)

  (2) \(3\)
  (3) \(2\)

## Section: Systems of equations (learn)

### VIDEO: Systems of Equations (3.2 min)
  --- slide 1: Systems of Equations
  [on screen] Massive title — "Systems of Equations"
  SAYS: Last lesson: one equation. Now: a system — two equations, sometimes even more.
  SAYS: These show up mainly in algebra questions.
  SAYS: The difficulty here is technical, not conceptual. So let's master the techniques.
  --- slide 2: Two equations
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] $x+y=11$
  [board] $2x-y=7$
  SAYS: Two unknowns, two equations. We need values that make BOTH true at the same time.
  SAYS: The first one alone has endless pairs. The second one narrows it down to one.
  (does: Next to them write "2 unknowns, 2 equations ✓")
  SAYS: Rule of thumb: as many equations as unknowns — and you can solve it.
  SAYS: Two main methods: substitution, and elimination. Same answer either way.
  --- slide 3: Substitution
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — x + y = 11 and 2x − y = 7
  [board] $x+y=11 \qquad 2x-y=7$
  SAYS: Method one: isolate a letter in one equation, and plug it into the other.
  (does: Under x + y = 11 write "y = 11 − x")
  SAYS: From the first: y equals eleven minus x.
  (does: Write "2x − (11 − x) = 7")
  SAYS: Put that WHOLE expression into the second equation, in place of y. The brackets matter — the minus hits both terms.
  (does: Write "2x − 11 + x = 7 → 3x = 18 → x = 6")
  SAYS: Two x minus eleven plus x equals seven. Three x is eighteen. x is six.
  (does: Write "y = 11 − 6 = 5")
  SAYS: Back to y equals eleven minus x: y is five.
  SAYS: Tip: if they ask for x, isolate y. It disappears — and you're left with exactly the letter you want.
  --- slide 4: Elimination
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — x + y = 11 and 2x − y = 7
  [board] $x+y=11 \qquad 2x-y=7$
  SAYS: Method two — often faster: add or subtract the equations.
  (does: Circle +y and −y)
  SAYS: Look — plus y and minus y. Opposites.
  (does: Write "(x + y) + (2x − y) = 11 + 7")
  SAYS: Add the two equations, left to left, right to right.
  (does: Write "3x = 18 → x = 6")
  SAYS: The y's cancel. Three x equals eighteen. x is six.
  SAYS: Same answer as substitution. Of course.
  SAYS: Same sign instead of opposite signs? Then subtract — and keep the whole second equation in brackets.
  --- slide 5: Match coefficients
  [on screen] Pre-loaded — 2x + 3y = 19 and 3x + 2y = 16
  [board] $2x+3y=19 \qquad 3x+2y=16$
  SAYS: Nothing cancels yet. So we MAKE it cancel.
  SAYS: Multiply whole equations until one letter has the same coefficient.
  (does: Write "×3" next to the first and "×2" next to the second)
  SAYS: First equation times three, second times two.
  (does: Write "6x + 9y = 57" and "6x + 4y = 32")
  SAYS: Every term gets multiplied — including the right side. Six x plus nine y is fifty-seven. Six x plus four y is thirty-two.
  (does: Write "5y = 25 → y = 5")
  SAYS: Now subtract: six x minus six x is gone. Five y equals twenty-five. y is five.
  (does: Write "2x + 15 = 19 → x = 2")
  SAYS: Back into the first: two x plus fifteen is nineteen. x is two.
  SAYS: Which letter should you match? The one they DON'T ask for — so it cancels and leaves the one you want.
  SAYS: Choose the smallest multipliers you can. Less arithmetic, fewer mistakes.
  --- slide 6: Ask what they want
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Sometimes $x+y$ is enough.
  [board] You may not need $x$ and $y$ separately.
  SAYS: One more thing before the questions.
  SAYS: Sometimes the question doesn't want x or y at all — it wants a combination, like x plus y.
  (does: Circle "x + y")
  SAYS: Then adding or subtracting the equations can hand you that combination directly.
  SAYS: So always circle what they ask for — before you start solving.
  --- slide 7: Recap
  [on screen] Blank
  [board] Substitution: isolate, then plug in
  [board] Elimination: add or subtract to cancel a letter
  [board] Multiply WHOLE equations to match coefficients
  SAYS: Let's lock it in.
  (does: Underline "WHOLE")
  SAYS: Two system questions next. Try each one — then watch how we solve it.

### Q 2 (q-164): Given: \(6\,x + 3\,y = 36\), \(x + y = 7\). \(x = ?\)
  (1) \(5\)
  (2) \(4\)
  (3) \(2\)
  (4) \(1\)

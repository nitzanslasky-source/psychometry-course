### Q 1 (wp21-g004): A game begins with 2 large crystals. Breaking a large crystal replaces it with 4 small crystals. Breaking a small crystal removes it. After exactly 3 breaks, which crystal count is possible?
  (1) 4
  (2) 5
  (3) 6
  (4) 7
  correct: 3
  solution: The first break has to hit a large crystal, because there are no small ones yet. One large crystal disappears and four small ones appear. We now have one large and four small: five crystals in total.
  solution: For the second break, choose the remaining large crystal. It becomes four more small crystals. Now all eight crystals are small. The third break removes one of them, leaving seven. That gives a complete legal sequence, so seven is possible.
  solution: We can also explore the other branch. If the second break removes a small crystal, we have one large and three small. Breaking the large next gives seven; breaking another small gives one large and two small, or three total. So the only possible totals are three and seven.

### Q 2 (wp21-g005): A queue contains at least one delivery van of length 5 m and at least one scooter of length 2 m. There is a 1 m gap between consecutive vehicles and no gap counted at either end. Which total queue length is impossible?
  (1) 8
  (2) 11
  (3) 14
  (4) 10
  correct: 3
  solution: Start with the required van and scooter. Their lengths plus the single gap total 5+1+2=8 metres, so eight is possible. Each extra scooter adds its own two metres and one new gap: three metres. Adding one or two scooters gives eleven and fourteen.
  solution: Ten lies between the minimum eight and the next possible total eleven. An added van would contribute six metres, which is even larger. We cannot add only a gap or part of a vehicle, so ten is impossible.

### Q 3 (wp21-g007): A mentor shares 32 pins among apprentices. Each apprentice receives a different number of pins (a positive integer), and all pins are distributed. What is the largest possible number of apprentices?
  (1) 6
  (2) 8
  (3) 9
  (4) 7
  correct: 3
  solution: To reach as many apprentices as possible, avoid using too many pins on the early recipients. Give one to the first, two to the next, then three, and so on. The running totals are one, three, six, ten, fifteen, twenty-one, and twenty-eight. Seven apprentices can so receive distinct positive amounts.
  solution: An eighth would need at least eight pins in addition to the first seven minimum amounts. That would make thirty-six, more than the available thirty-two. We have proved eight is impossible.
  solution: Seven is not just an upper bound: use 1,2,3,4,5,6,11. They total thirty-two, are all positive, and are all different. The four pins left after the minimum distribution can safely go to the person who already has the most.

### Q 4 (wp21-g009): A training drill begins with 46 repetitions. In each later round, the athlete does half the previous round’s repetitions, then subtracts 1. How many repetitions are done in round 4?
  (1) 6
  (2) 4
  (3) 3
  (4) 5
  correct: 1
  solution: Round one is already forty-six; do not apply the rule before recording that starting value. Round two is half of forty-six, twenty-three, minus one: twenty-two. Round three is half of twenty-two, eleven, minus one: ten. Round four is half of ten, five, minus one: four.
  solution: We apply the rule three times to move from round one to round four. A fourth application would answer round five instead. Also keep the operation order: halve first, then subtract one. Halving forty-five would describe a different rule.

### Q 5 (wp21-g010): A charity receives 3 crates on day 1, and twice as many as the previous day on every later day. How many crates are received in total through day n?
  (1) \(3n^2\)
  (2) \(3\times2^n\)
  (3) \(2^n+3\)
  (4) \(3(2^n-1)\)
  correct: 3
  solution: Choose n=3 so the actual sequence is short. The daily counts are three, six, and twelve, for a total of twenty-one. Now test all four formulas at the same chosen n. The proposed expression 3(2^n−1) gives 3(8−1)=21. The alternatives give twenty-seven, twenty-four, and eleven.
  solution: Only one formula survives this legal test, so it is the correct choice among the listed answers. A single example does not prove an identity in general, but it can eliminate the wrong answer formulas. Using n=1 may leave accidental ties; three remains easy while separating these choices.

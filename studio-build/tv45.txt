# Topic 45 Parables and comparisons

######## SECTION vr45-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-comparison-method: kind=lesson title='Parables and comparisons: match the roles'
[1] Translate the story into a relationship
  BOARD: ['Who acts?', 'What are they trying to achieve?', 'What goes wrong or stays the same?']
  SCRIPT: A speaker may answer a practical argument with a story about a gardener, a ship or a broken road. The objects are not usually the point. Ask who acts, what they are trying to achieve, and which relationship the comparison highlights. If someone criticizes painting over a damp wall without fixing the leak, the point is treating a visible symptom while leaving its source. An answer about a gardener trimming damaged leaves while ignoring the cause may fit even though it contains no paint or building. We are looking for the same reasoning pattern under different scenery.
[2] Give every important role a partner
  BOARD: ['Problem ↔ problem', 'Attempted response ↔ attempted response', 'Reason the response fails ↔ same reason']
  SCRIPT: Map the roles one at a time. The leak is the continuing source of the problem. The stain is its visible effect. Repainting is a response to the effect that leaves the source untouched. A good parallel must preserve those roles and their connection. A distractor may mention a wall too but describe a completely different issue, such as choosing an expensive color. Shared nouns are not enough. In harder questions, two options may both concern prevention, but only one preserves the timing or the dependence that made the original comparison work.
[3] Find what the speaker is answering
  BOARD: ['Read the claim before the comparison.', 'Is the reply agreement, criticism or qualification?']
  SCRIPT: A comparison gets its force from the claim it answers. Suppose a manager wants to reward a team before the project is completed. A colleague says this is like counting fruit before the tree has flowered. The reply challenges treating an uncertain future benefit as already secured. If the preceding claim instead concerned choosing the wrong kind of tree, the intended point would be different. Read the short conversation as a conversation, with a natural change of voice between speakers. Then paraphrase the reply without any of its story objects. That abstract sentence becomes your answer test.
[4] Do not add an extra lesson
  BOARD: ['The comparison makes one relevant point.', 'Do not import every property of its objects.']
  SCRIPT: A ship may have a captain, a crew, an engine and a destination. A comparison involving a ship does not automatically use all four. If the speaker compares a leader following public opinion to a person walking in front of a crowd while copying its turns, the relevant issue is apparent leadership versus actual direction. The weather at sea or the skill of sailors has nothing to do with that point. Keep the map as small as possible. Adding properties from your own experience can make a wrong option seem rich and convincing while moving away from the text.
[5] Watch the direction of cause
  BOARD: ['People gather where music is played.', 'Does music attract them, or do performers choose crowds?']
  SCRIPT: Comparisons can test causal direction. A busy place may attract performers rather than become busy because performers arrived. Both accounts connect the same two things, so an answer that simply mentions the association does not preserve the distinction. Draw the arrows in each story. If the original reply reverses an assumed cause, the correct parallel should reverse an assumed cause too. This connects the comparison topic with strengthening and weakening: we are looking underneath a familiar story to see which inference is being challenged.
[6] Check the closest distractor
  BOARD: ['Same problem?', 'Same timing?', 'Same dependence?']
  SCRIPT: After finding a plausible parallel, compare it with the closest rival. One may describe prevention before a problem occurs; the other repairs damage afterwards. One may reward a result before it is secured; the other merely make a plan in advance. Planning is not the same as pretending the plan has succeeded. A few precise contrasts like these let you reject an attractive answer without a long essay. During practice, write the abstract relationship in a single sentence. Under time pressure, keep that sentence in your head and test the roles in the same order.

=== VIDEO solve-vr45-g001: kind=solution title='Appearance is not behavior'
[1] Your question
  BOARD: []
  SCRIPT: When a director claimed that a stern-looking applicant would surely be a strict supervisor, a colleague replied, “A painted tiger cannot frighten the mice out of a granary.” What is the colleague’s main point?
[2] Put the message in plain English
  BOARD: ['An intimidating appearance does not establish effective behavior.']
  SCRIPT: A painted tiger resembles something intimidating without doing what a real predator does. The comparison challenges the leap from appearance to supervisory behavior. Strip away the objects in the story and state its lesson about the relationship. Then put the real situation back into those roles. If a proposed answer needs an extra fact that neither story supplied, the comparison does not justify it.
[3] Keep each answer tied to the text
  BOARD: ['Appearance and function']
  SCRIPT: “All supervisors should look friendly.” fails because this is a new rule about appearance, not the point of the comparison. “The applicant has never seen a tiger.” fails because literal knowledge of tigers is irrelevant. “The granary needs a different kind of paint.” fails because paint is part of the illustration, not a proposed workplace solution. We have a reason for rejecting each alternative. That matters more than whether an answer repeats a familiar word from the passage. A strong distractor often borrows the vocabulary while changing the owner, direction or strength of the claim. Return to the exact part of the text that decides between the closest two options.

  BOARD: ['Choice 3', 'An intimidating appearance does not establish effective behavior.']
  SCRIPT: The answer is choice 3: An intimidating appearance does not establish effective behavior.. A painted tiger resembles something intimidating without doing what a real predator does. The comparison challenges the leap from appearance to supervisory behavior.

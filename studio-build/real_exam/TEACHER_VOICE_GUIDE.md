# Teacher Voice Guide: how she really talks (for future scripts)

Based on 64 recorded English math videos (topics 1-14, 34,300 spoken words), each compared with its studio script (v19-hybrid). Every example below is a real quote from a transcript.

## 0. The numbers

| | Script | What she actually says |
|---|---|---|
| Words | 1 script word | **about 3 spoken words** (median 3.0x, range 0.9x-6x) |
| Length | planned "minutes" | **about 2.2x the planned time** (solutions: median 2.5 min, lessons: 3.5 min) |
| Pace | | **about 167 words per minute** (range 123-198) |
| Sentence length | about 7 words, many fragments | about 12 words, full sentences joined with *so / because / and therefore* |
| "right?" | 0 | **354 times**, about once every 35 seconds |
| "Choice N" | 165 times | 11 times. She says *"number three is our answer"* |
| "is equal to" | 1 | 308 |
| "to the power of" | 0 ("three to the twelfth") | 171. She never uses the ordinal form |

She is fluent. The scripts aren't too hard for her. They're too **compressed**, so she unpacks every line herself. Write the unpacked version.

## 1. Openings and closings

- **Lessons:** *"Welcome back, and now roots."* / *"Welcome to Advanced Exponents and Roots."* / *"Okay, we're done with fractions."* / *"We're done with all the basic rules. Now the traps the exam actually loves."*
- **Solutions:** she **reads the question aloud first**, in almost every video, even though no script line asks her to: *"A water tank holds 240 liters. On Monday, 3/8 of the water is used..."* Other openings: *"Here's a beautiful question..."*, *"Here's a tricky one."*, *"Okay, I have here no numbers at all, just signs."*
- **Closings:** *"I'll see you in the next lesson / question / video."* (23 times). This often comes after a short nudge: *"Try both methods, and then watch the solutions."* / *"So take the time memorizing these."* / *"So please rewatch this if this wasn't clear."* / *"Yes, it's hard and it's tiring, but it's worth it."*
- At the end of a solution she says *"And that's it."* / *"Perfect, I got to my answer."* / *"Therefore, 3 is our answer."* / *"We could circle two and move on."*

**Rule:** start a solution script with the stem in plain words. End with one practical nudge plus "I'll see you in the next question." Drop logistics lines like "Next: a card with the wordings" or "Two guided questions next". She skips them.

## 2. Her signature moves

1. **She asks a question and answers it straight away, in the first person.**
   *"What's 24 divided by 8? Three."* / *"When is x times y negative? Only when they have opposite signs."* / *"Now how do I match the bases?"* / *"So what does that mean?"* (18 times) / *"What do I do?"*
   It's always "how do **I**", never "how do you".
2. **"right?" as a check-in after each fact.** *"9 is equal to 3 to the power of 2, right?"* Also *"Okay?"* and a standalone *"Okay,"* to move on.
3. **She narrates the board.** *"I have here"* (77 times), *"over here"* (76), *"this whole thing"* (27), *"to the other side"*, *"this side"*. She always **says the expression out loud** before working on it. Scripts often rely on the board and don't say it, so she adds it every time.
4. **She explains why.** *"Why is this? Because..."* comes after almost every rule. Examples: why a root can't hold a negative ("no number times itself gives a negative"), why 0.2³ = 0.008 (count the decimal places), why equal bases means equal exponents (*"if 2^x = 2^4, x has to be 4"*).
5. **She adds a number example to every abstract rule.** *"Let's see this in numbers."* / *"Let's take two random numbers."*
6. **She names things with an image.** *troublemakers* (0, 1, -1, ½, 10), *fake primes*, a prime's *ingredients*, the *root world*, *under one umbrella*, a perfect square *hiding* inside a root, *mirror* equations (5x+3y / 3x+5y), *99 copies of 41*, the *tie-breaker* number, *benchmarks*, *the big picture* vs *high-school math* (*"from our high school math we're used to finding x, then y. In the psychometry, usually that's not needed."*).
7. **She labels each method.** *"So that was our first method. Our second method would be to plug in numbers."* / *"We're going to try to solve this with three different methods, starting with algebra."* / *"Okay, let's try to do this in our first way."*
8. **She does the mental arithmetic out loud and breaks it into easy steps.** *"30 times 44, how did I do that? Well, what's 10 times 44? 440. Now 440 times three..."* / *"2 plus 2, 4 plus 2, 6 plus 2, 8."*
9. **She plugs in the choices in order and eliminates them.** *"Not 8. I could cross it out... Can I already circle it and move on? I can't, because I may have another one."* / *"So I'm going to now move on to my next easiest option."*
10. **She uses a Hebrew-style "also... and also".** *"I'm dividing also the left side and also the right side."* This is her voice, so keep it.

## 4. What she always adds (the scripts are missing these)

- **The stem, read aloud** (solutions). Example: q-386 *"X and Y are integers that are not prime. Okay, they're both not prime."*
- **A why line or a mini proof.** q-250 adds *"if I have 1 to the power of n equal to 1 to the power of 5, does that mean n is 5? No."* Roots adds *"why can't we have a negative in the root?"*
- **A second number example.** q-389 (GCD) adds a full worked example with 172 and 112 (about 60% of the video). t13-tools redoes |a−b| = |b−a| with 2 and 7. advanced-powers adds 27⁴ = 3¹² and 64 = 4³. t03-summary adds 4/5 → 8/9 vs 9/8 → 12/11.
- **The general pattern behind the trick.** t06-03 explains "mirror" equations with an extra example (2x+4y / 4x+2y) before adding them. t05-shortcuts: *"It means I have 99 copies of 41."*
- **Study habits.** "Memorize the primes, or this would take you a long time and very likely you'd make a mistake." / "try to do each question in multiple methods" / *"Try to pause for a moment and try to see if you find a way..."*
- **The common wrong move, up front.** t08-01: *"The first mistake I want to take off from here: it's just not equal to 8 to the power of n."*

## 5. What she skips or shortens

- **"Choice three."** She almost never says it (165 in scripts, 11 spoken). She says *"number three"* / *"so three is our answer"*.
- **Trap lines** ("Choice two is the trap. It comes from..."). 25 scripts have one, and she voiced something similar in only 6. If a trap matters, put it **before** the solution as a warning in her style ("A lot of students will think... but..."), not as a post-answer list.
- **"Notice:" and closing tips** like *"Never forget to try seven"*, plus meta lines (*"Later in the course..."*, *"Next: a card..."*). Usually dropped.
- **Final checks** that are long lists (t14-01 cut the 2/3/5/7 tests of 113 down to one line).
- **Telegraphic fragments** ("Monday: three eighths OF 240. Of means times."). She rebuilds these into sentences every time.
- **CAPS emphasis** (REMAINING, SMALLEST, NOT). She can't voice the caps. She says it with words instead: *"We have to watch for the word remaining."*

## 6. Where she stumbles

There are very few real restarts. They cluster in four places:
- **Compressed formula lines:** q-295 *"21 plus six plus 21 plus six plus 21. Times two times six, right? Because I have two times square, sorry..."*
- **Long chained expressions read from the board:** q-250 *"3 minus 3 to the power of minus 6 plus 6x"*, *"1 over 27, sorry 81"*.
- **Comparisons of two absolute values:** q-358 *"No, sorry the opposite."*
- **Improvised generalizations come out slightly wrong** when the script gives no exact sentence. Examples: q-250 *"that only works when they're both positive"* (it means the base). t03-summary *"the smaller the numerator, the bigger"* (it means the denominator). t08-traps *"with a negative fraction it's the opposite"*. She will generalize anyway, so **write the exact general sentence for her.**

## 7. Rules for every new script

1. **Write full spoken sentences of 8-15 words**, joined with *so, because, and therefore, now*. Avoid colons, em-dashes, "X? Y." fragments and caps.
2. **Solutions start with the stem** in plain words, then *"So what are they asking?"*
3. **Each step has three parts.** Say the expression, then ask how ("How do I...?"), then do it, then add *right?* Example: *"Now I have 6 over 6 over 5, right? What's 6? 6 is 6 over 1."*
4. **Give every rule a why and a number example** (*"Let's see this in numbers."*).
5. **Name methods:** "our first method..., our second method...". Name tricks with an image (mirror, troublemakers, tie-breaker).
6. **Answer line:** "So number three is our answer." Never "Choice three."
7. **Traps go before the answer as a warning,** at most one per video.
8. **Math wording:** "to the power of", "is equal to", "over", "in absolute value". Expand every formula term by term.
9. **Plug-in method:** pick the easiest legal number and say why ("Why did I choose a quarter? Because its root is a half."). Check the choices in order and cross out. If two survive, use a "tie-breaker" number.
10. **Close with one nudge** ("try both methods", "memorize these") and "I'll see you in the next question."
11. **Plan for length.** About 55 script words per finished minute when she reads it. A tight 130-word solution script becomes about 2.5 min. If you write in her voice from the start, assume about 160 words per minute.
12. **Let her be.** Don't script "Okay", "right?" or "I have here" more than about once per beat. She adds them herself, and a script full of them reads as fake.

## 8. Notes for the AI voice (ai_scripts/*.json)

Measured on the 1,257 lines of ai_scripts/r26-*.json:
- **Too many pauses:** 12% of lines contain "..." ("Bars on the small side... the answer is trapped"). She almost never pauses mid-sentence. She bridges with *so / because / right?* Replace most "..." with a comma or "so".
- **No "right?" at all, and no first person.** Her two strongest markers are missing. Use about one "right?" per slide and an occasional "I" ("If I square it, I get...").
- **"Good." 26 times, and "Well," starts 26 lines.** She says "Perfect" or nothing. Her "well" belongs mid-thought ("Well, let's test it"), not as a line opener.
- **Telegraphic Q-fragments** ("Bars equal a number? Then you have two cases. Plus, and minus."). These are the same compression she rewrites when she reads. Use full sentences of 10-12 words.
- **Math wording should match her:** "to the power of", "is equal to", "1 over 27", "number three" instead of "choice three".
- **Style field:** "calm, natural hesitations" doesn't fit her. Better: "warm, brisk (about 165 wpm), talks to one student, rhetorical question then immediate answer, few pauses".
- **Settings:** memory says voice "Nitzan website 1" + eleven_v4, but ai_scripts/_voice.json still says PVC "Nitzan PVC" + eleven_multilingual_v2. Align them before generating more.

## Appendix: sample rewrite of an unrecorded topic-16 solution (solve-q-458)

**Question:** p, q and r are consecutive even numbers, and 0 < p < q < r. (r² − p²) / 2q = ?  The choices are 1, 2, 4, 8 and the answer is 3 (= 4).
Same beats, same methods, numbers and draw cues. Only the `say` lines change. The course files were not edited.

### Method 1 · Algebra

| # | Before (script) | After (her voice) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Consecutive EVEN numbers — let's use algebra. | So p, q and r are consecutive even numbers, and they're all bigger than 0. And we're asked what's r squared minus p squared, all that over 2q. |
| 2 | Heads-up: bigger than zero doesn't mean they start at two. | Now, bigger than 0 doesn't mean they start at 2, right? They could be 10, 12 and 14. So let's do this first with algebra. |
| 3 | Write all three using the middle one, q. Consecutive evens — gaps of two. | What does consecutive even numbers mean? Each one is 2 more than the one before, like 4, 6, 8, right? So I'll write all three of them using the middle one, q. |
| | *[draw] Write "p = q − 2, r = q + 2"* | *(unchanged)* |
| 4 | p is q minus two. r is q plus two. | p is 2 below q, so p is equal to q minus 2. And r is 2 above q, so r is equal to q plus 2. |
| | *[draw] Write "(q + 2)² − (q − 2)²" over 2q* | *(unchanged)* |
| 5 | Substitute. Keep the second bracket — there's a minus in front of it. | Now I'll put these in. On the top I have q plus 2, all that squared, minus q minus 2, all that squared. And on the bottom I still have 2q. I'm keeping the second one in brackets, because there's a minus in front of this whole thing. |
| | *[draw] Write "= (q² + 4q + 4) − (q² − 4q + 4)"* | *(unchanged)* |
| 6 | Open with the contracted multiplication formulas. Only then remove the brackets. | Now I open each one with the formula. q plus 2, squared, is q squared plus 4q plus 4. q minus 2, squared, is q squared minus 4q plus 4. And only now I take off the brackets, so the minus flips every sign inside, right? |
| | *[draw] Write "= 8q", then "8q / 2q = 4", and circle choice 3* | *(unchanged)* |
| 7 | The q squareds cancel, the fours cancel. Eight q over two q — four. Choice three. | So what cancels? q squared minus q squared, gone. 4 minus 4, gone. And 4q plus 4q is 8q. So I have 8q over 2q. The q's cancel, and 8 over 2 is 4. So number three is our answer. |
| 8 | Choice two is the trap. It comes from gaps of one — but these are even numbers. The gap is two. | Now, if you got 2, you probably used a gap of 1, like 4, 5, 6. But these are even numbers, so the gap is 2. |

### Method 2 · Plugging in numbers

| # | Before (script) | After (her voice) |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | Or plug in the smallest legal numbers: two, four, six. | Now our second method: plugging in numbers. What are the easiest numbers I'm allowed to use? 2, 4 and 6, right? They're even, they're consecutive, and they're bigger than 0. |
| | *[draw] Write "(36 − 4) ÷ (2 · 4) = 32 ÷ 8 = 4"* | *(unchanged)* |
| 10 | Thirty-six minus four: thirty-two. Two times the middle: eight. Thirty-two over eight: four. | So r squared is 6 squared, that's 36. p squared is 2 squared, that's 4. 36 minus 4 is 32. And on the bottom, 2 times the middle one, 2 times 4, is 8. 32 over 8 is 4. |
| | *[draw] Circle choice 3* | *(unchanged)* |
| 11 | Four — choice three. | Same answer, 4, number three. Two different methods for the same question, so try both of them. I'll see you in the next question. |

**What changed:** the stem is read aloud (line 1). The colons, em-dashes and caps are gone. Every expression is said in full, with the formula expanded term by term (line 6, where she stumbled in q-295). There's a "why" for the gap of 2 with a number example (line 3), and a reason for choosing 2, 4, 6 (line 9). "right?" appears about once per beat. The trap is told as "if you got 2..." (line 8). "Choice three" became "number three". It ends with a nudge and her closing. The script grew from about 130 to about 330 words. Expect about 2-2.5 minutes, close to what she would produce anyway, but now the wording is checked.

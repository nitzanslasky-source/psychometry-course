Many people believe that regular physical exercise improves a person's emotional state. In recent decades numerous studies have attempted to verify this belief, and their findings confirm that a connection exists; but, to the considerable surprise of the researchers, the connection turned out to be a weak one. In Denmark, for example, membership of sports clubs doubled in the course of the 1990s, yet the level of well-being that Danes reported in the same period did not rise in most cases, and even where a rise was recorded, it proved to be temporary. Similar results were obtained in Canada and in Japan, in surveys that followed tens of thousands of participants over several years.

Why does exercise exert so limited and so transient an influence on well-being? "Reference-point theory" can supply part of the answer. Its central claim is that people evaluate their condition primarily by comparing themselves with those around them, so that their well-being is determined first of all by how fit they are relative to others, rather than by their fitness in itself. According to the "peer hypothesis," which forms part of the theory, the more people regard others as their equals, the more they compare themselves with them; a person is unlikely to measure himself against a professional athlete, but very likely to measure himself against a colleague of his own age. This may explain why an improvement in fitness barely affects well-being. Shortly after a person joins a club, the group with which he compares himself changes: it now consists of the experienced members who train alongside him, and most of them are fitter than he is. His fitness has improved, but his position relative to his new reference group has, if anything, deteriorated.

An alternative explanation attributes the phenomenon to habituation. According to this view, people rapidly become accustomed to an improvement in their condition and soon regard it as a matter of course. Habituation raises the standard that a person expects of himself, and his expectations advance in step with his achievements, so that the distance between the two — which is what determines his satisfaction — remains approximately constant. A runner who once celebrated completing five kilometers will, within a few months, be dissatisfied with anything less than ten. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive, and several researchers have proposed that they operate simultaneously: comparison determines how a person evaluates his achievement at any given moment, while habituation determines how long the evaluation endures.

A study conducted in the Netherlands in 2014 attempted to distinguish between the two explanations. Its participants, none of whom had exercised regularly before, joined clubs and were followed for two years. Half of them trained in groups, and half trained alone with a personal instructor, so that the second half had no fellow members with whom to compare themselves. If comparison were the whole explanation, the participants who trained alone should have reported a lasting improvement in well-being; in fact their improvement faded almost as quickly as that of the others. The researchers concluded that habituation accounts for most of the phenomenon, and comparison for the remainder — though they conceded that a participant training alone may compare himself with acquaintances outside the club, so that the distinction between the two groups was less clean than they had intended.

Whatever the mechanism, the practical conclusion is the same, and it has been drawn by the promoters of public health with some reluctance: exercise is to be recommended for its physical benefits, which are well established, and not for its emotional ones, which are not. As the proverb has it: "Who is strong? He who is content with his strength."

For a long time scholars of literature assumed that the question of what makes a text a poem admits of a definite answer. According to the classical view, a poem is defined by a fixed set of characteristics, so that for any text one can determine, without hesitation, whether it is a poem or not. The model for such a determination was the sonnet: a sonnet has fourteen lines, and no text of fourteen lines fails to be a sonnet on that account, while no text of thirteen qualifies. The classical scholars believed that the category of the poem in general could be defined with the same precision, provided that its characteristics were correctly enumerated, and they devoted considerable effort to enumerating them.

This view has attracted extensive criticism. First, the critics argued, the boundaries between the categories of texts are not sharp at all. Where exactly, for example, does the boundary between prose and poetry lie? Can one point to a text that is prose but not yet poetry — a text on one side of the boundary, with its nearest neighbor on the other? The critics maintained that no such boundary can be located, and that the categories shade into one another by imperceptible degrees. Second, they argued, even where characteristics can be enumerated, they are not all necessary. The classical definition of a poem, for instance, appeared to require that a poem rhyme, that it maintain a regular meter, that it be divided into lines, and that it concern the emotions. Yet a text that does not rhyme, maintains no meter, runs continuously as a single line, and concerns a bicycle is nevertheless recognized as a poem by every competent reader.

The scholar Ruth Aviram proposed a different account of how readers classify texts. In her view we do not decide whether a text is a poem by checking it against a list of necessary characteristics, but by comparing it with a "model" — a typical, familiar example of the category. When we classify a text, we set it beside the model, and if it resembles the model sufficiently we count it as a poem; the more it resembles the model, the more readily we do so, and the less it resembles the model, the more we hesitate. Aviram's account explains what the classical view could not: why readers agree about the central cases and disagree about the marginal ones, and why a text can be "more of a poem" than another without either being excluded from the category. It also accommodates the sonnet, which had been the classical scholars' favorite illustration: a sonnet is simply a category whose model is unusually specific, so that resemblance to it is unusually easy to assess. Experimental evidence has subsequently supported the account. When readers were presented with unfamiliar texts and asked to classify them, their responses to the central examples were rapid and unanimous, whereas their responses to the marginal examples were slow, hesitant and inconsistent — precisely the pattern that classification by resemblance predicts and classification by enumeration does not.

Aviram's proposal has not gone unchallenged. Its critics have observed that the model itself must be selected by some criterion, and that the criterion, whatever it is, reintroduces the definition that the proposal was meant to dispense with. Aviram's reply was that the model is not selected at all but acquired: a reader learns what a poem is from the poems he encounters first, and his model is simply the residue of that encounter. This reply, however, raises a further difficulty, since readers whose first encounters differ should then possess different models and classify texts differently — which, as far as anyone has been able to determine, they do not.

Between the seventeenth and the nineteenth centuries, the governments of Europe undertook the careful mapping of their territories. Surveyors were dispatched into the mountains to fix the positions and heights of passes, summits and springs. The methods of surveying available at the time were limited, however, and one of them in particular produced error after error, as the surveyors themselves were the first to admit. The consequences were serious. Roads were constructed at gradients that no vehicle could climb; bridges were designed for valleys whose depth had been misjudged; and more than one expedition, relying on a map that placed a pass a thousand feet lower than its true elevation, perished among the ridges.

The landscape that a surveyor observes in high mountains is virtually the same wherever he stands, summit succeeding summit to the horizon. Surveyors therefore relied on two measurements: the horizontal distance from a known point, and the elevation above sea level. The first of these could be measured with reasonable precision, and the surveyors of the period took pride in the accuracy they achieved. Using a chain and a carefully measured baseline, and observing the angles from either end of the baseline to the summit, a surveyor could calculate the distance to a point he was unable to reach. The elevation could not be determined in a similar way. In the mountains nothing within the surveyor's field of vision stands at a known height: the horizon itself is inclined by the slopes, and every summit is measured against neighboring summits whose elevations are as uncertain as its own. Surveyors were therefore obliged to rely on a chain of measurements ascending from the coast, in which a minor inaccuracy at each stage accumulated into a large one by the summit.

It had long been recognized that elevation can be calculated from atmospheric pressure, which diminishes as one ascends. For every hundred meters of ascent, the reading of a barometer decreases by about one unit. All that is required is to compare the reading obtained on the mountain with the reading recorded, at the same moment, at a station of known elevation. The obstacle lay in the instrument. The barometers of the period consisted of a column of mercury in a glass tube. Such an instrument could not be transported up a mountainside without spilling or shattering, and its readings fluctuated with the temperature, so that the comparison with the station's reading was of little value. Several expeditions nevertheless carried mercury barometers into the Alps, and the elevations they published for the same summit differed from one another by hundreds of meters, so that a map compiled from them was worse than no map at all.

The absence of a reliable method troubled France in particular. Its Alpine frontier was the most contested in Europe, and its military engineers required accurate maps of every pass through which an army might advance. In 1826 the Academy of Sciences in Paris announced a prize for a portable barometer of proven reliability. The prize was awarded to the instrument-maker Lucien Vidi, who had proposed to dispense with mercury altogether. His "aneroid" barometer measured the pressure by means of the expansion and contraction of a sealed metal capsule from which the air had been evacuated. The capsule neither spilled nor shattered, and it was almost insensible to cold, so that its reading on the mountain could be compared with the station's reading with confidence. Within a generation the elevations of the principal ranges of Europe had been established by survey parties equipped with Vidi's instrument, the discrepancies between rival maps had been settled, and the maps compiled from the new measurements remained in use, with only minor corrections, for a century.

One of the great unresolved questions of biology is why human beings laugh. Laughter appears in every culture, begins in infancy, and seems to serve no obvious purpose. Until recently the accepted view was that laughter's principal function is the discharge of tension: laughing reduces the body's physiological stress and enables it to recover from strain. This view rests on numerous findings. In a study conducted at the University of Bern, two groups of participants were required to perform a stressful task; afterwards one group watched a comedy and the other a documentary about glaciers. The concentration of stress hormones measured in the comedy group declined more rapidly than in the other group. Dozens of similar studies, conducted with different tasks and different measures, report a reduction of approximately 15 percent in various indicators of stress following laughter, and the finding has been regarded as one of the most reliable in the field.

Recently the researcher Miriam Adler proposed an alternative explanation. The effect of laughter on stress, as documented in the studies, is small — too small, in her opinion, to be regarded as the principal function of a behavior so universal. She argues, moreover, that laughter is not necessary for the discharge of tension at all: if it were, every social animal would need to laugh, whereas numerous species never laugh and recover from strain no less efficiently than we do. She also reinterprets a well-known experiment from the 1970s, in which volunteers confined for two weeks in isolation, without company and therefore without laughter, exhibited a sharp rise in stress. The cause, she argues, was not the absence of laughter but the isolation itself. A recent experiment supports her interpretation: volunteers kept in company but prevented from laughing for a month — an instruction they reported as irksome but manageable — exhibited no rise in stress whatsoever, and their physiological measurements remained indistinguishable from those of a control group permitted to laugh freely.

In order to understand laughter, Adler maintains, one must examine not the body but the group — the circumstances in which each species lives. This approach, she claims, accounts for the considerable differences in laughter between individuals and between species. Why, for instance, do people laugh thirty times more frequently in company than alone, and why do they laugh most readily at remarks that are not amusing at all? Because, according to Adler, laughter is a signal transmitted to others: it informs the group that the individual laughing is at ease and intends no harm, and it thereby reduces the likelihood of conflict and spares the members of the group the effort of vigilance. "Within a group, the optimal strategy for an individual who wishes to retain his place is to laugh whenever he is not occupied with something more important," she writes. "Laughter is a kind of flag of truce." The apes that laugh most are those that live in the largest groups, in which quarrels are most frequent and most costly; and among human infants, laughter is directed almost exclusively at the caregivers on whose goodwill the infant depends, and appears only when the infant is old enough to distinguish them from strangers. Adler regards both observations as evidence that laughter is calibrated to the audience rather than to the state of the individual who laughs.

Adler's theory is now being tested by means of a new instrument: a miniature recorder, worn on the collar, that registers every laugh over a period of weeks together with an indication of whether anyone else was present. If the theory is correct, laughter in solitude should prove to be as rare as a flag raised in an empty field. Whether it is, the recordings will reveal.

In 1904 the draining of a peat bog near the Danish village of Hjortlund uncovered a wooden chest containing some six hundred thin wooden tablets. A discovery of this kind is exceptional. Wood survives from antiquity only where air cannot reach it, and the bog, which had submerged the tablets in acidic water for two thousand years, had preserved them. The preservation, however, had come at a considerable price. The tablets had been inscribed by cutting fine marks into their surface with a stylus, and in the course of two millennia the marks had contracted until they were barely perceptible. When the first tablets were lifted from the bog and dried, moreover, the wood warped and split. Most of what had been inscribed on those tablets was irretrievably lost. The tablets are virtually the only documents of the northern Iron Age composed by its inhabitants themselves rather than about them by Roman observers, and this accounts for the extraordinary efforts that have been devoted to deciphering them.

The development of infrared photography in the twentieth century revived the hope of reading the tablets. Infrared photography is sensitive to the composition of a surface. Different substances reflect infrared radiation in different proportions; a substance that reflects little appears dark in the photograph, and one that reflects much appears light. Since the ink employed on ancient documents was usually manufactured from soot, which reflects almost no infrared radiation, writing that has faded to invisibility in ordinary light frequently stands out with remarkable clarity in an infrared photograph. In recent decades this technique has recovered the text of faded parchments discovered in Egypt and in the Judean desert, and of manuscripts damaged by fire in European libraries, and scholars anticipated that it would accomplish the same for the tablets of Hjortlund.

The marks on the tablets of Hjortlund, however, had been cut into the wood rather than written upon it; no ink whatsoever had been used. The surface of a mark and the surface of the wood adjacent to it were therefore the same substance. They reflected infrared radiation in precisely the same proportion, and in an infrared photograph the two were indistinguishable. The technique that had proved so effective elsewhere was, for these particular tablets, entirely useless, and after a series of disappointing trials in the 1960s the attempt was abandoned. The tablets were returned to storage in sealed containers of water, and for half a century the chest of Hjortlund was regarded as a treasure that could be preserved but never read.

Then, in 2019, a team from the University of Aarhus succeeded in reading several of the tablets by means of a technique known as RTI. RTI likewise depends on photography, but it registers a different property of the surface: not its composition but its relief. The tablet is photographed dozens of times in succession, illuminated on each occasion from a different direction. A computer then combines the photographs into a single image. In this image every ridge and every hollow, however shallow, casts a shadow that shifts with the direction of the light. The technique had been developed for the study of coins and seals, whose designs are worn but not erased; its application to the tablets was proposed by a graduate student who had noticed that the marks, though invisible, could be felt with a fingertip. A mark cut into the wood, although it reflects light exactly as the surrounding wood does, is a hollow — and in the combined image its edges emerge as shadows. Reading a single tablet in this manner requires several days, and the team has so far recovered only a few dozen lines. It is confident, nevertheless, that the technique will eventually yield the contents of the entire chest.

It is natural to assume that a young songbird acquires its song from the adult birds it hears around it. The song a bird produces as an adult is, after all, the characteristic song of its own species. This assumption, however, does not accord well with the well-documented fact that young birds produce phrases they have never heard. This difficulty has occupied a prominent position in the study of birdsong for more than sixty years. It is, in miniature, the same difficulty that confronts the student of human language, and the two disciplines have borrowed their arguments from each other repeatedly and productively.

The modern study of the subject originated in the critical review that the zoologist William Thorpe published in 1958 of the earlier accounts, which had attributed the acquisition of song to imitation alone. Thorpe distinguished between two kinds of input that a young bird may receive, which he called "exposure" and "feedback." Exposure consists in hearing the adult song. Feedback consists in information that a phrase the young bird has sung is defective. According to Thorpe, exposure by itself cannot teach a bird the rules by which the phrases of its species' song are combined. Since adult birds scarcely ever respond to a young bird's errors, the bird's knowledge must have an additional source. Thorpe designated this source the "song template." The template, in his conception, is an innate mechanism in the bird's brain that contains the rules common to the songs of all songbirds. The bird draws on it when it produces phrases it has never heard. The hypothesis was attractive because it accounted, at a stroke, for the two facts that imitation alone could not reconcile: the fidelity of the adult song to the species, and the novelty of the phrases in the young bird's repertoire.

Although Thorpe's approach gained widespread acceptance, evidence has accumulated over the past few decades that undermines its assumptions. It transpires, for instance, that adult birds do supply feedback, but indirectly. An adult female that hears a young male sing responds with a brief, characteristic fluttering of the wings. She flutters in the majority of cases in which the phrase was well formed, and in only a small minority of the cases in which it was not, so that the response itself can indicate an error. Other studies have found that the more frequently a phrase is sung in a young bird's surroundings, the earlier it appears in the young bird's own repertoire. Young birds raised in silence, by contrast, sing as adults a song so impoverished and monotonous that it barely qualifies as the song of their species. These findings underlie the "practice-based" school. It explains the acquisition of song comprehensively by general capacities of learning that every bird possesses — imitation, trial, and generalization from examples — and does not require the assumption of a template.

Criticism of Thorpe's theory has also arrived from another direction: the assumption that there exist rules common to the songs of all species, on which the "song template" depends, has proved exceptionally difficult to confirm. No such rule has been identified. Moreover, even the terminology employed to describe the rules — "syllable," for instance, or "refrain" — is derived predominantly from the study of the songbirds of Europe, and in many cases corresponds poorly to the songs of birds elsewhere at all. An ornithologist who attempted to describe the songs of the Australian lyrebird in these terms reported that the attempt produced a description of European birds rather than of the bird before him. Whether universal rules of song exist therefore remains an open question. So long as it remains open, the practice-based school has not prevailed, since it too has yet to demonstrate that general capacities of learning are sufficient.

Every animal requires food, and almost all the food on land derives, at one remove or another, from plants; but the plants themselves depend on something considerably less conspicuous. Before a plant can utilize the minerals in the soil, dead organic matter must be decomposed and its minerals released, and this is the indispensable function of the soil's bacteria. The process, which proceeds invisibly and continuously beneath every field and forest, is the first link in the chain of fertility. The bacteria decompose leaves, wood and the bodies of animals; the minerals they release are absorbed by the roots of plants; and the plants in turn nourish the animals. The greater the bacterial population a soil contains, the more fertile it is.

Several environmental factors limit the abundance of soil bacteria and affect their distribution. Temperature is one of them: bacterial activity is almost completely suspended in frozen soil. Acidity is another, though only in unusual soils, such as those of peat bogs or of certain volcanic slopes, where it may suppress bacterial activity almost entirely. Salinity, similarly, suppresses bacteria in coastal soils and in irrigated fields where evaporation has concentrated the dissolved salts, but it is a local phenomenon rather than a general one.

The principal factor limiting bacteria in the soils of arid regions is moisture. Bacteria require water in order to be active, and require a steady supply of it in order to survive. The quantity of water in a soil determines not only how many bacteria inhabit it but also of what kinds. In soils that dry out for prolonged periods there are few bacteria, of exceptionally hardy varieties that revive rapidly and complete their work in the brief interval after rain. In humid regions, by contrast, bacteria are abundant and extraordinarily diverse, and there the principal factor limiting them is not water but the supply of dead organic matter to decompose.

In the soils of the humid tropics, however, the limiting factor is neither water nor organic matter, but minerals — principally potassium and phosphorus, which are comparatively scarce in most soils and are continuously consumed by the organisms that inhabit them. In the tropics the heavy rains carry the minerals downward, along with the fine particles of clay to which they adhere, into a layer of soil far beneath the roots. This layer is effectively inaccessible to the bacteria and to the roots, since almost all of them inhabit the topmost soil, the only layer that air penetrates. Because the minerals descend and do not ascend, the topmost soil is chronically deficient in them, and the forest that grows on it is poorer than its impressive dimensions would suggest. There are exceptions, however, wherever the deep soil is brought to the surface: on the mounds that termites construct from soil they carry up from far below, and in the fields of farmers who dig deep before planting. In such places, and only in such places, the soil is rich in minerals, and the crops that grow on it are renowned throughout the surrounding region. Agricultural scientists have attempted to reproduce the effect artificially by ploughing to a depth of two meters, but the expense has generally proved prohibitive.

The distribution of soil bacteria across the earth therefore exerts a profound and frequently underestimated influence on the distribution of all life, since it determines the fertility of the soil and, through it, the quantity of plant and animal life a region can sustain. Soil bacteria consequently rank among the most important living things in the ecological balance of the world — for the minerals they release are the foundation of every form of life on land.

The barcode, first employed at a supermarket checkout in 1974, is not one of them. Unlike the great inventions, it involved no technological breakthrough whatever. Economists who have attempted to quantify that influence estimate that the barcode has saved the retail trade of the industrialized countries several hundred billion dollars, and the estimate is probably conservative.

Every item that arrived at a shop had to be marked with its price manually. Every sale had to be registered by a clerk reading the mark. A shop that stocked many kinds of merchandise required many clerks, and consequently most shops stocked few kinds; a grocer of the 1960s who carried more than a thousand distinct items was regarded as extravagant, and possibly reckless. The barcode replaced this entire apparatus with a single operation: the code printed on the item is read by a scanner, and the price, the sale and the inventory are recorded simultaneously, without a clerk touching a pen. The saving was immediate and enormous: within five years of the first installation, the labor expended on pricing in the supermarkets of the United States had diminished by two thirds.

The fundamental revolution, however, lay not in the barcode itself but in its standardization. For a scanner to read a code, every code must be constructed in the same manner; and for a code printed by a manufacturer in one country to be read in a shop in another, the manner must be identical throughout the world, and enforced by an authority that every manufacturer acknowledges. Establishing a universal standard acceptable to all the participants was a matter of dispute for years. The countries that had already begun to employ codes of their own were the losers, since a change of standard obliged them to replace every scanner and every label in use. Japan, for example, which had printed codes of eight digits on millions of items, was obliged to reprint them all with thirteen; and the manufacturers of scanners in the United States, whose machines read codes of twelve digits, were obliged to reconstruct them to read the additional digit that the rest of the world had adopted. The negotiations were conducted by committees of engineers, but their outcome was determined predominantly by the commercial weight of the participants, and the standard eventually adopted was, unsurprisingly, the one favored by the largest retailers.

Like many inventions, the barcode has not benefited everyone. Small shops that could not afford scanners lost their customers to the chains that could, and tens of thousands of them closed within a decade; in France alone, the number of independent grocers fell by half between 1975 and 1990; the clerks who had registered sales lost their employment permanently; and the record that the scanner retains of every purchase has generated persistent controversy about how much a retailer should know about the people who buy from it. Whether the gain is worth the cost is a matter of opinion. That the barcode matters is not.

The pursuit of a universal doll, Feld concluded, was misconceived, because no such doll exists. The company, which had invested heavily in the project, was reluctant to accept the conclusion, and commissioned two further studies before conceding that Feld was right.

Feld's conclusion has since been confirmed independently for numerous toys; but one toy is the exception: the teddy bear. Although manufacturers have attempted for a century to sell bears of various kinds, a single design satisfies almost everyone, irrespective of age, sex or nationality. The story begins in the 1900s, when stuffed toys were sold with eyes of glass mounted on wire pins, which worked loose and were occasionally swallowed by infants. The embroidered eyes could not be detached; but they also gave the bear a new face — round, close-set and level — and customers, it transpired, preferred it overwhelmingly. Manufacturers who experimented with alternatives — bears with elongated faces, bears with glass eyes restored, bears with painted smiles — abandoned them within a season.

One explanation, proposed by developmental psychologists, is that it possesses, in balance, all five of the characteristics to which small children respond in an object: it is soft to the touch, it can be held in both arms, its face is symmetrical, its eyes are large in proportion to its head, and its expression is neither happy nor sad. Most toys possess two or three of these characteristics. A doll with a laughing face has a fixed mood, which the child cannot alter to suit his own; a plastic animal, however appealing its proportions, cannot be embraced. The bear possesses all five, and none of them to excess. The explanation accounts for the failure of the experimental alternatives: each of them sacrificed one characteristic in the attempt to enhance another.

An alternative explanation invokes the tendency of small children to reject whatever is unfamiliar — unfamiliar food, unfamiliar places, unfamiliar people. This tendency presumably protected children in the past, when the unfamiliar was frequently dangerous; but today it chiefly obstructs them. Parents, however, have discovered a practical use for the bear. Taken along to the first day at nursery or to the doctor's waiting room, it converts an unfamiliar place into one that contains something familiar, and the child accepts what he would otherwise have refused. Nursery teachers report that the children who arrive with a bear settle within days, while those who arrive without one may take weeks. The bear, in other words, is not merely a toy but an instrument by which parents circumvent a tendency that evolution has left them unable to switch off.

Until the 1970s students of communication unanimously held that every gesture a person makes conveys information, and that it can therefore be accurate or inaccurate. This assumption was overturned by the publication of "Gesture and Action" by the anthropologist Adam Kendon. Kendon argued that the traditional view does not fit every kind of gesture. Gestures that describe a state of affairs — "descriptive gestures," in his terminology — can indeed be accurate or inaccurate. A hand held at knee height to indicate the height of a child either corresponds to the child's height or does not. But consider a hand raised in an auction room. This gesture describes nothing. It is not an attempt to convey a fact, and it therefore cannot be accurate or inaccurate. Rather, it makes a bid — it alters the state of affairs instead of reporting it. Gestures of this kind Kendon designated "active gestures." The distinction, he noted, cuts across the conventional classifications of gesture, since a single movement — a raised hand — may be descriptive in one situation and active in another.

According to Kendon, an active gesture cannot be accurate or inaccurate, but it can succeed or fail. In order to succeed, that is, to alter the state of affairs, it must satisfy what Kendon called "conditions of success." It must be made, for example, in a setting in which the convention on which it relies is in force, and it must be made by someone entitled to make it. A raised hand constitutes a bid only in an auction room, during the sale, by a person registered to bid. If a spectator raises his hand, or a bidder raises it after the hammer has fallen, the gesture alters nothing, however distinctly and deliberately it was made. A further condition is sincerity: a person who raises his hand in bid must intend to pay. Kendon observed that the conditions are seldom stated explicitly, and that people acquire them, as they acquire the conventions of their language, without deliberate instruction.

After Kendon had formulated the distinction between the two kinds of gesture, however, he came to reject it. He argued that the informational content of a gesture is insufficient to characterize it completely. Every gesture, according to Kendon, is composed of three layers: the **movement**, that is, the motion of the hand or the body itself; the **act** performed by means of the movement, such as pointing, requesting, warning or bidding; and the **effect** the gesture exerts on those who observe it, on their thoughts and on their subsequent conduct and decisions. It follows that every gesture is, in a sense, an active gesture, since every gesture performs some act. Kendon replaced the distinction between descriptive and active gestures with a distinction between explicit and implicit active gestures — gestures in which the act performed is immediately evident, and gestures in which it must be inferred from the circumstances.

To illustrate Kendon's method of analysis, consider the following illustration. At dinner, Dana wants the salt, which stands beside Yosi. She looks at Yosi and inclines her head toward the salt. The movement is the inclination of the head accompanied by the glance. The act performed is a request — the inclination is made in a setting in which Dana's plate is unsalted and Yosi is nearest to the salt, and this setting indicates what act the movement performs. The effect is whether Yosi passes the salt or does not. Dana's gesture is an implicit active gesture, and it corresponds to the explicit gesture of pointing at the salt and then at herself, which performs the same act by a movement whose meaning requires no setting to be understood. The analysis applies, Kendon maintained, to every gesture whatever, from the diplomatic handshake to the referee's whistle, and it is for this reason that his three layers have been adopted throughout the discipline.

Pranayama is an ancient Indian method of treating illness by the regulation of the breath. According to the Indian tradition on which it draws, a vital force — prana — circulates in the body along channels called nadis. Illness, according to this doctrine, results from an obstruction or an imbalance in the circulation of prana, and the breathing exercises of pranayama are intended to clear the channels and restore the equilibrium. The exercises themselves are elaborate, and a full course of them may occupy an hour each day and requires months of instruction.

At the beginning of the twentieth century Indian teachers began to propagate pranayama in the West, and for some years it enjoyed a vogue in Europe and America, adopted by, among others, several prominent writers, at least two members of the British aristocracy, and a small number of physicians. In the 1930s, however, the vogue subsided, principally because of the increasing authority of Western medicine, which regarded pranayama as superstition. In the same years the standing of pranayama declined in India as well, under the influence of the medical schools the British had established there. It might have been forgotten altogether but for the independence of India in 1947, after which the new government promoted the traditional practices as part of the national heritage — and because they were inexpensive, and the new state could not afford Western medicine for a population of three hundred million. Nehru himself, who headed the government, did not believe in pranayama. "I have no faith in it," he wrote to a colleague, "but the people have, and we need them healthy."

It appears, then, that the fortunes of pranayama depended on social circumstances rather than on its efficacy. Whether it was practised in a given decade tells us what the decade thought of Indian tradition, and nothing whatever about whether the exercises work. In the 1970s Western medicine, which had dismissed it, began to subject it to controlled trials. Many of the trials detected an effect, and in 1978 the World Health Organization published a list of conditions — among them elevated blood pressure and asthma — for which breathing exercises were judged to be beneficial. The mechanism nevertheless remained a puzzle, since terms such as prana and nadis have no meaning in Western physiology. The trials could establish that patients who performed the exercises improved; they could not establish why, and the Organization's list was accordingly published with a note conceding that the mechanism was unknown.

Although Western science found no explanation for the therapeutic power of pranayama, a few scientific explanations of its capacity to reduce blood pressure were proposed. According to one, slow and deep breathing stimulates a nerve that runs from the brain to the heart and slows the heartbeat. But this explanation is also in doubt, since the slowing of the heartbeat persists for minutes only, whereas the reduction in blood pressure that the trials detected persisted for weeks. An effect that outlasts its supposed cause by such a margin, the critics observed, must have some other cause.

The doubt is not confined to the mechanism. Many scientists attribute the entire effect of pranayama to the placebo effect — the improvement that derives not from the treatment itself but from the patient's belief in it and his expectation of relief. If they are correct, the trials that detected an effect were defective, since a trial in which the patient knows that he is being treated cannot separate the treatment from his belief in it. How, then, did the World Health Organization accept them? The suspicion arises that the influence of India, which in those years led the bloc of non-aligned states, and from whose eyes the Organization wished to conceal its poor opinion of Indian medicine, played its part.

The biologist perceives in the floor of the deep sea a region of exceptionally sparse and peculiar life. The geologist perceives in it a plain of slowly accumulating sediment. The engineer perceives in it a place of crushing pressure and perpetual and unvarying cold. But the most conspicuous characteristic of the deep sea floor is its poverty of food, since it underlies all the phenomena that these specialists observe. In the absence of light nothing grows there, and every organism that inhabits it must subsist on what descends from above. The scarcity of food is therefore the critical factor, which determines more decisively than anything else the appearance of the deep sea floor and the ways of life of its inhabitants.

A distinction should be drawn between food that arrives distributed thinly and uniformly over a wide area and food that arrives concentrated in a single place. The first kind is the continuous, almost imperceptible drizzle of minute organic particles that sink from the surface. The second kind — the "food fall" — is the carcass of a large animal, a whale or a great fish, that sinks intact to the floor: enormous, infrequent, and entirely unpredictable. Most of the food that reaches the deep floor arrives in the second manner, and this imposes considerable hardship on the animals that live there. An animal that has grown, or reproduced, on one fall requires food in order to continue living; and in order to surmount the difficulty the animals of the deep floor have "adapted" to the conditions that the food falls create.

The irregularity of the food falls is both spatial and temporal. Spatially, a fall does not spread over a wide area but lands at a single point, and there is no predicting where the subsequent one will land. It may be said that the floor immediately surrounding a fall, a minute fraction of the whole, receives all the food. The animals of the deep floor respond to this in two ways. Some — those incapable of traveling far — congregate permanently at the few locations where food arrives steadily, in the vicinity of the vents of warm water on the floor, and live there in remarkably dense colonies. Others, which are capable of traveling, roam the empty plains between the vents, and when a fall lands they converge on it from considerable distances, reproduce on it, and disperse again when it has been consumed. The two strategies are not interchangeable: a colony animal transplanted to the plains starves, and a roaming animal confined to a vent ceases to reproduce.

As stated, there is irregularity in time as well. The interval between one fall and the next is not fixed, and years may elapse in which no fall whatever lands. The roaming animals must be prepared to reproduce on any fall they reach, since they may never reach another; but they do not reproduce on every fall. Reproduction is initiated only by a fall exceeding a certain magnitude — a quantity that varies with the region — a threshold — since the young require food for months after they hatch, and a small fall is consumed before they can mature. Moreover, the threshold is not uniform: it is higher in the emptiest basins than in the richer ones. In the emptiest basins a small fall is less likely than elsewhere to be followed by another before the young starve, and reproducing on it would be a wager with distinctly unfavorable prospects. Regarded in this way, the animals of the deep floor resemble a speculator who assesses, before every investment, the probability of a return, and a visitor to the deep might suppose that nature there is as calculating as the market. But that would be an illusion. The animals calculate nothing; natural selection has simply eliminated, invisibly and inexorably, over the generations, those that wagered on falls too small to repay them.

In 1861 the German scholar Hugo Bergmann published a study of the hymns of the Rigveda, the most ancient of the Indian scriptures, composed more than three thousand years ago. The most surprising of his numerous findings concerned smell. The hymns describe sacrifices, feasts and fields in considerable detail. Nevertheless they contain almost no words for smells. The smoke of the altar is "bitter," the flowers of the meadow are "sweet," the fat of the offering is "heavy." Moreover, the few words that do concern smell are employed in ways that appear to us thoroughly peculiar: the smell of fresh butter, for example, is described as "cold." Bergmann argued that these descriptions were composed by people whose sense of smell was less developed than ours. The reason for this, in his view, is that their sense of smell had not yet fully evolved.

Bergmann's claim was an audacious one: the theory of evolution, on which it depended, was still in its infancy, and the notion that a sense could develop over generations appeared distinctly far-fetched. But the claim was not abandoned. The philologist Otto Kranz undertook to investigate it a few years later. Kranz examined the words for smells in the oldest texts of many cultures. In the oldest of them the only words for smells are "foul" and "fragrant." In later texts words for particular smells appear — "smoky," "sour," "of meat." The last to appear, in every culture, are words for faint smells such as "musty" and "earthy." Kranz maintained that the order reflects a modification of the nose that occurred over the generations. He thereby went considerably further than Bergmann. The physiological component of his claim has since been abandoned; but the order of emergence he identified is still accepted, and it has been the point of departure of every subsequent investigation of the subject.

In the twentieth century anthropologists began to test the claim that the source of the phenomenon is physiological. They examined peoples whose languages possess comparatively few words for smells. Their preliminary findings appeared to support Bergmann and Kranz. All the languages possessed words for "foul" and "fragrant"; most possessed words for a few particular smells; and only some possessed words for faint smells. When a member of a people whose language had no word for "musty" was asked what an old cellar smells of, he answered "foul" or "of earth." The anthropologists then presented the members of such peoples with a set of scented papers and asked them to sort the papers by smell. They sorted the musty specimens apart from the foul and the earthy without the slightest hesitation. Their noses, evidently, distinguished what their vocabulary did not. The experiment has since been repeated with several unrelated languages, and the result has proved consistent: vocabulary constrains description, not discrimination.

Why, then, did they describe the cellar as foul or earthy? The linguist Asifa Majid explains it as follows: in a culture in which a smell has no name, it is entirely natural to assign it to a neighboring name — foul, for instance, or earthy — much as a person with no word for "turquoise" will call the color blue or green. If perception is not the issue, however, why the fixed order? Majid explains that as follows: since foulness is the smell of spoiled food, of sickness and of danger, every culture required a name for it urgently, and required it early. The faint smells, by contrast, seldom possessed comparable significance. A person can pass through life without ever needing to distinguish musty from earthy — and until people began to store grain and wine in cellars, the overwhelming majority did precisely that.

The everyday interpretation of the term "nonsense" is words devoid of significance, or matters that are simply impossible. Those who occupy themselves with the literary genre bearing that name, however, do not regard its works as devoid of significance. Works of nonsense are not devoid of significance or of logic; they are founded on rules of their own, and a reader who fails to identify those rules will indeed find them unintelligible.

The most conspicuous characteristic of works of nonsense is a deliberate deviation from accepted logic, and an implied challenge to what is taken for granted. A celebrated illustration appears in "Alice in Wonderland," in which the March Hare offers the following argument: "You should say what you mean." "I do," Alice replies, "at least I mean what I say — that's the same thing, you know." "Not the same thing a bit!" says the Hare. The reasoning is founded on an entirely coherent principle — that reversing the order of a relation does not preserve it — and it is deployed against a proposition that everyone accepts without examination. The Hare, in other words, is not talking nonsense in the everyday sense at all; he is reasoning more rigorously than his interlocutor.

A further characteristic of works of nonsense is the invention of words and the assignment of new significations to existing ones. In the verse of Lea Goldberg, for instance, the speaker declares: "…a fool, a dolt, an utter clot / no letter and no line has got." A second illustration is the poem of Lewis Carroll named for the creature "Jabberwock," a word he invented. The poem employs invented words of good phonetic construction, and produces from them lines that a reader parses without in the least understanding. There are works of nonsense in which the invented words assume a still greater role, and the reader must infer their significations from the surrounding lines alone, as an archaeologist infers the meaning of an inscription from its context.

Certain works of nonsense possess a graphic characteristic as well: the words are arranged on the page so as to form a shape. In the poem of Morgenstern entitled "The Night Song of the Fish," the central portion of the composition consists entirely of two graphic symbols — a dash and an arc — arranged on the page in the shape of a fish. The reader who encounters it must decide whether he is reading a poem, contemplating a drawing, or doing both simultaneously. Morgenstern himself declined to say, and the composition has been reprinted in anthologies of poetry and in anthologies of graphic art alike.

Works of nonsense employ familiar signs, but combine them in unexpected and unaccustomed ways. The adult reader finds it difficult to interpret them, since he has already learned to read the world in a particular manner, and the boundaries of his imagination are consequently narrower. Children, by contrast, have not yet learned what may not be done, and they accept the invented word, the reversed relation and the fish composed of dashes without protest. Most of the adults who occupy themselves with a work of nonsense attempt to extract from it a coherent significance and are frustrated when they fail; children, who expect no such thing, are amused. The difference is not one of intelligence but of expectation.

It may be asked, accordingly, whether the literature of nonsense is a variety of children's literature. The answer is not unequivocal. There are works of nonsense that assist a child in learning the rules of language and of logic by violating them conspicuously, and thereby drawing attention to their existence. But there are works of nonsense that presuppose a reader who already knows the conventions intimately, since only such a reader can recognize precisely which convention is being violated. Works of that kind, although they employ the apparatus of children's literature, are addressed to adults.

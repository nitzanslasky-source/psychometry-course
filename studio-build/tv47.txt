# Topic 47 Scientific reasoning

######## SECTION vr47-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-science-method: kind=lesson title='Scientific reasoning: what did the comparison show?'
[1] Five things to keep separate
  BOARD: ['Question · hypothesis · design · result · conclusion']
  SCRIPT: Scientific questions in this course are reading and reasoning questions. You do not need to arrive with specialist knowledge of the invented plants or devices in an example. Keep five things separate. The question asks what we want to know. The hypothesis proposes an explanation or predicts a relationship. The design describes how we compare cases. The result is what was observed. The conclusion says what the researcher thinks the result supports. A mechanism is a further account of how an effect occurs. Mixing these roles can make a very ordinary paragraph feel much harder than it is.
[2] What changes, and what is measured?
  BOARD: ['Change: amount of shade', 'Measure: seedling growth', 'Keep other relevant conditions comparable.']
  SCRIPT: Suppose a hypothetical study asks whether shade changes seedling growth. The amount of shade is the factor being changed. Growth is the outcome being measured. Put an arrow from the proposed influence to the outcome, but remember that it is still a hypothesis until we examine evidence. If shaded plants also receive more water, the comparison changes two things at once. A difference in growth would then be difficult to attribute specifically to shade. This is why we need comparable conditions. We want a design that makes the proposed influence easier to separate from other plausible influences.
[3] A control can be zero or another level
  BOARD: ['No added shade versus shade', 'Light shade versus heavy shade', 'The comparison should answer the actual question.']
  SCRIPT: A control group does not always mean doing nothing at all. To test whether added shade matters, we might compare no added shade with some shade. To compare two amounts, we might use light shade and heavy shade. Choose the comparison that answers the question. If we want to know whether a new training program is better than the usual program, comparing it only with no training can miss the relevant difference. Both groups should receive the other important parts of the experience in a comparable way. Otherwise, attention, practice time or equipment might explain the result.
[4] Random assignment helps; it is not magic
  BOARD: ['Random assignment reduces systematic selection.', 'Larger groups help balance chance differences.', 'Check what else changed after assignment.']
  SCRIPT: Random assignment makes it less likely that one group starts with systematically different motivation, experience or ability. It does not guarantee that two small groups are perfectly identical. Nor does it prevent a later difference if one group also gets extra time or different instructions. So do not treat the word ‘random’ as an automatic answer to every design problem. Read what the groups actually received and what was measured. A fair comparison is an argument about which rival explanations have been reduced, not a decorative label attached to an experiment.
[5] Support does not mean proof
  BOARD: ['A predicted result supports a hypothesis.', 'A rival may predict the same result.', 'One study has limits.']
  SCRIPT: If a result matches a prediction, it can support the hypothesis. It does not automatically prove that the hypothesis is the only correct explanation. Another account may predict the same result. If a result fails to match a precise prediction, it creates a problem for that account, but we should also ask whether the relevant conditions were actually present and the measurement worked. For exam questions, use the facts supplied rather than inventing endless possible errors. The main correction to keep in mind is simple: evidence changes the strength of a claim; a favorable finding is not automatically a final proof.
[6] A useful test separates the rivals
  BOARD: ['Account A predicts improvement in both groups.', 'Account B predicts improvement in only one.', 'Compare the place where the predictions differ.']
  SCRIPT: Suppose one explanation says an exercise helps because of the extra practice time. Another says the particular kind of exercise matters. Give two groups equal extra practice time with different exercises. Now the explanations make different predictions. Testing two conditions in which both accounts predict the same thing may add data without settling the dispute. Write a tiny prediction table if needed. Under account A, what should happen? Under account B, what should happen? The best comparison is often the one where those rows separate, not the one with the most elaborate equipment.
[7] The same result can need a different explanation
  BOARD: ['More output may mean faster work.', 'Or more workers, easier tasks, longer hours.', 'Check the unit being compared.']
  SCRIPT: When a study reports an increase, ask increase in what unit. Total output, output per worker, and output per hour are different measures. A larger group may produce more while each person produces less. The same applies to counts of errors, repairs or complaints. Compare the outcome to the opportunity for that outcome to occur. You do not need heavy calculation to see the issue. Often naming the denominator is enough. If the question supplies exact numbers, calculate carefully; if it does not, do not quietly assume equal exposure.
[8] Explain both parts of a puzzling result
  BOARD: ['What was expected?', 'What was found?', 'Does the proposed mechanism fit every stated part?']
  SCRIPT: An explain-the-finding question often gives a result that seems surprising: more help but worse performance, fewer reports despite more problems, or an improvement in one group only. State the whole puzzle before reading the options. A proposed explanation that accounts for only half of it may be incomplete. For example, if an audience helps on a practiced task and hurts on an unfamiliar one, ‘an audience always helps’ does not fit both findings. We need an account whose effect changes with the relevant task feature. Active reading helps us keep both halves in view instead of choosing an option that merely echoes one phrase.

=== VIDEO solve-vr47-g001: kind=solution title='A factor hidden inside a comparison'
[1] Your question
  BOARD: []
  SCRIPT: In a hypothetical study, students who practiced a puzzle in the morning performed better than students who practiced it late at night. The researcher attributes the difference to time of day. Which information most weakens this interpretation?
[2] Put the message in plain English
  BOARD: ['The morning group had slept much longer before the practice session.']
  SCRIPT: Sleep differs alongside time of day and offers another explanation of performance. The other facts make the groups more comparable. Label the factor, the measured outcome and the proposed explanation. If two hypotheses are involved, write what each predicts for the same situation. The deciding evidence is where their predictions differ, not a detail that both explanations can comfortably accept.
[3] Keep each answer tied to the text
  BOARD: ['Confounding variable']
  SCRIPT: “Both groups solved the same puzzle.” fails because a shared puzzle controls task differences. “Both groups received the same instructions.” fails because shared instructions reduce an alternative explanation. “The groups were tested for the same length of time.” fails because matched testing time improves the comparison. We have a reason for rejecting each alternative. That matters more than whether an answer repeats a familiar word from the passage. A strong distractor often borrows the vocabulary while changing the owner, direction or strength of the claim. Return to the exact part of the text that decides between the closest two options.
[4] Choose the matching answer
  BOARD: ['Choice 2', 'The morning group had slept much longer before the practice session.']
  SCRIPT: The answer is choice 2: The morning group had slept much longer before the practice session.. Sleep differs alongside time of day and offers another explanation of performance. The other facts make the groups more comparable.

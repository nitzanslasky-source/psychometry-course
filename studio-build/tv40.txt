# Topic 40 Analogies

######## SECTION vr40-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-analogy-method: kind=lesson title='Analogies: start with the word that needs the other'
[1] A relationship, not a shared subject
  BOARD: ['time : clock', 'A clock is an instrument that measures time.']
  SCRIPT: Time and a clock are connected, but ‘connected’ is far too broad to solve an analogy. We need a sentence that tells us how they are connected. Start with the word that is easier to define through the other word. Here that is clock. A clock is an instrument that measures time. Time does not need a clock in order to exist, but our definition of a clock needs the idea of time. This is what we mean by the dependent word. We are looking for a useful defining relationship, not debating whether an object could physically exist in a world without its partner. Our sentence now gives us a clear test for another pair.
[2] Keep the printed order
  BOARD: ['time : clock', 'temperature : thermometer', 'Second word measures the first.']
  SCRIPT: We started the definition with clock, the second word, but we have not reversed the printed pair. Our rule is still: the second thing measures the first. Temperature and thermometer fit that order. A thermometer measures temperature. Thermometer and temperature would put the roles in the wrong places. You can draw an arrow from the word you define to the word you use in its definition. What matters is that you carry exactly the same roles into the answer choices. Starting with the dependent word makes the sentence easier to form; it does not give us permission to rearrange a pair when we check it.
[3] Try the definition with every role intact
  BOARD: ['hunger : meal', 'A meal can satisfy hunger.', 'A medicine may relieve a symptom; a diagnosis identifies it.']
  SCRIPT: Try hunger and meal. It is easier to define the meal’s role through hunger than to define hunger through a meal. A meal can satisfy hunger. Notice the word ‘can.’ Not every meal completely satisfies every person, so our relationship should not make an unnecessary guarantee. Now compare a remedy and a diagnosis. A remedy aims to relieve a problem; a diagnosis identifies it. Both belong to the same general world of problems and responses, but their jobs differ. We want the closest relationship, not just two words from a similar subject. A good definition is precise enough to make that distinction and modest enough to stay true.
[4] When dependence is not obvious
  BOARD: ['scarce : plentiful', 'Opposite ends of one scale', 'Do not force a tool or purpose into every pair.']
  SCRIPT: Sometimes neither word clearly depends on the other. Scarce and plentiful are opposites. There is no need to invent a story about one producing the other. Use the dependent-word approach as your first question: which word can I define naturally through its partner? If that does not reveal a useful direction, test another simple relationship: opposites, similar meanings, stronger degree, part and whole, action and result, or prevention. The method should make the pair clearer. It should never force you into an awkward sentence just because every previous example began with a tool. We will practice a range of relationships so that the first question becomes a flexible habit.
[5] A true definition can still be too broad
  BOARD: ['an archive : documents', 'An archive keeps documents.', 'A forest contains trees, but it is not a storage facility.']
  SCRIPT: ‘An archive contains documents’ is true, but a forest contains trees and a box contains shoes too. If more than one answer fits, sharpen the sentence. An archive is a place or collection organized to preserve records. That gives us something more useful than ‘contains.’ Do not add irrelevant details simply to force a favorite option. We do not need to specify the archive’s opening hours or the color of its shelves. We refine the relationship itself: storage, preservation, membership, material, cause, or purpose. A useful check is to ask whether your extra detail belongs to the meanings of the words, rather than to one example you happened to imagine.
[6] Cause, purpose and result differ
  BOARD: ['warning → aims to prevent harm', 'injury → harm has occurred', 'Do not turn an intention into a guaranteed result.']
  SCRIPT: A warning is intended to help someone avoid a danger. It does not guarantee that the person will listen. An injury, by contrast, tells us that harm has occurred. This difference is easy to lose when we define every pair with a vague verb such as ‘makes.’ Ask whether the second word is the aim of an action, its necessary result, a possible result, or a state the action tries to prevent. The English exam pairs often rely on exactly that small distinction. Your relationship sentence can use ‘aims to,’ ‘can,’ ‘results in,’ or ‘prevents’ as appropriate. Choosing the right verb is part of solving the question.
[7] Small part or weak degree?
  BOARD: ['a crumb : a loaf', 'a drizzle : rain', 'A component is not the same as a mild form.']
  SCRIPT: A crumb is a small piece of a loaf. A drizzle is a light form of rain. Both suggest something small, but they do not have the same structure. One is a component of a larger object; the other is a weaker degree of a phenomenon. A letter and a word would fit the component relationship, not the intensity relationship. When two choices seem close, name the difference in plain English. ‘A piece of’ and ‘a mild form of’ are already enough. You do not need a technical label before you can solve the question. The labels simply help you remember a distinction you have understood.
[8] Let the pair choose the meaning
  BOARD: ['eye : needle', 'An eye is the opening through which the thread passes.', 'Do not keep an unrelated sense of eye.']
  SCRIPT: English has words with more than one meaning too. In eye and needle, eye does not mean a seeing organ. The partner word selects the relevant sense: the small opening through which thread passes. The relationship in the pair helps us choose among possible meanings. Here is the reasoning move: select the sense that makes a precise relationship, then keep that sense while checking the answers. If every choice fails, revisit the definition before assuming that the vocabulary is impossible. Sometimes the words are familiar and the first interpretation was simply the wrong one.
[9] Use the choices to repair a definition
  BOARD: ['Define → test → refine', 'Change the rule for all choices together.', 'Never bend it separately for each answer.']
  SCRIPT: Usually, define the pair before looking closely at the answers. But if you cannot get a useful definition, the choices can suggest the kind of relationship you missed. Perhaps you called an anchor ‘something on a boat’ and then notice that the options all concern preventing an unwanted event. That can prompt a better definition: an anchor prevents a vessel from drifting. Once you revise the rule, apply the revised version to every choice. Do not give one answer ‘prevents,’ another ‘treats,’ and a third ‘detects,’ then say they all fit. That is three different rules. The goal is one sentence with the same roles and the same strength.
[10] Vocabulary without panic
  BOARD: ['Identify what you know.', 'Use word families and context cautiously.', 'Do not assume unfamiliar words are synonyms.']
  SCRIPT: A difficult-looking word may express a simple relationship, but unfamiliar words are not automatically synonyms of their partners. If you know one meaning confidently, use it. A word family can help, and the answer choices may help you recognize a sense, but mark any guess as a guess in your own thinking. During practice, keep a vocabulary note with the word, a short definition and a sentence showing the relevant sense. A bare translation is often less useful than that sentence. In a timed set, make the best supported choice and move on when further staring is not adding information. Afterwards, repair the vocabulary gap separately from the reasoning mistake.
[11] A short answer check
  BOARD: ['Same roles?', 'Same direction?', 'Same strength and specificity?']
  SCRIPT: Before choosing, run three quick checks. Does the answer keep the same roles: tool and task, producer and product, or problem and response? Does it keep the same direction in the printed pair? And does it preserve the strength and specificity of the connection? A possible result is not a guaranteed result, and a place where an action happens is not necessarily a place into which the action puts something. In early practice, explain why each distractor fails. Under time pressure, the explanation can be just a few words in your head. Those few words should identify a real mismatch, rather than simply saying that one answer feels better.

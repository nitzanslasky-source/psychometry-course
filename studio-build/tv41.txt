# Topic 41 Sentence completion

######## SECTION vr41-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-completion-method: kind=lesson title='Sentence completion: hear how the parts fit'
[1] Read a sentence, not a row of holes
  BOARD: ['Choose the completion that creates a coherent whole.', 'Read one option into all the blanks in order.']
  SCRIPT: A sentence with several blanks can look like a pile of disconnected fragments. Give yourself a complete version to work with: read an answer choice into the blanks in order. The first phrase goes in the first blank, the second in the second, and so on. You can start with the first option, or begin with an option whose opening you understand quickly. The important thing is to read a real sentence and follow its logic. Do not choose an answer because its first phrase sounds good. A later phrase may reverse the claim or make the example contradict the point it is supposed to illustrate.
[2] Keep a short skeleton
  BOARD: ['Long names → clear labels', 'Descriptions → keep only what matters', 'But, only, not and unless stay.']
  SCRIPT: Suppose a long sentence describes a historian with an unfamiliar name who wrote several books in a city with an unfamiliar name. If the issue is whether she accepts a particular explanation, your mental version can simply say ‘the historian accepts the explanation.’ But do not remove the word ‘partly,’ ‘only,’ or ‘not.’ Those words change the claim. Think of this as keeping the sentence’s skeleton: who takes which position, why, and what turns or limits that position. Use it to reduce memory load, not to flatten all the interesting logic out of the sentence.
[3] Predict the direction
  BOARD: ['Although X → expect a contrast', 'Because X → expect a reason that fits', 'For example → expect an instance of the claim']
  SCRIPT: Once the first clause makes sense, predict the direction of the next one. If the sentence says a device is reliable but something else is true of it, expect a limitation or a contrast. If it says ‘for example,’ the example should illustrate the claim just made. This is not a rule that contrast words always join a positive word to a negative word. ‘Although the team lost, the coach was pleased’ contrasts an event with an unexpected reaction. Read the ideas, not just plus and minus signs. Your prediction is a quick check: does this completion take the sentence where its connective promised to go?
[4] Read the turn with emphasis
  BOARD: ['The plan was CHEAP, yet INEFFECTIVE.', 'The plan was cheap and THEREFORE ATTRACTIVE.']
  SCRIPT: Use dramatic inner reading here. Let ‘yet’ sound like a turn and ‘therefore’ sound like a conclusion. In the first sentence, low cost comes with a disappointing limitation. In the second, low cost explains why someone finds the plan attractive. Saying the sentence naturally can expose a completion that looks grammatical but has the wrong relationship. Do not overdo the performance or read aloud during a timed exam. The point is a small shift in your inner voice that makes the link between clauses easier to hear. If a sentence still feels unclear, pause after the clause and give yourself a plain-language paraphrase.
[5] An example must demonstrate the point
  BOARD: ['Claim: predictions sometimes fail.', 'Example: a predicted rise became a fall.', 'A correct prediction would not illustrate failure.']
  SCRIPT: Here the claim is that predictions sometimes fail. The example should show a prediction that did not match what happened. If an option makes the example a perfectly accurate prediction, the sentence may be grammatical but it does not support its own point. This is one of the most useful checks for a long sentence. Ask what job the last clause is doing. Is it an example, a reason, a qualification, or an opposing view? A completion can fail because it fills the wrong job even when each separate phrase sounds reasonable. Internal coherence is the target; specialist knowledge about an actual historical forecast is not required.
[6] Counterfactuals: separate the imagined case
  BOARD: ['Had the guide arrived, the tour could have begun.', 'Imagined condition: guide arrives', 'Do not invent a complete history from this sentence.']
  SCRIPT: Counterfactual wording asks us to consider a situation different from the one being discussed. ‘Had the guide arrived’ means ‘if the guide had arrived.’ ‘Had it not rained’ means ‘if it had not rained.’ First rewrite that condition simply. Then read the result with its exact strength: would, could, or might. Be careful here: we should not automatically negate both halves and claim to know the whole real outcome. The sentence may leave another route to the outcome open. If the question explicitly tells us the guide did not arrive and the tour was canceled, those are facts we can use. Otherwise, keep the imagined relationship separate from unstated history.
[7] Untangle one negative at a time
  BOARD: ['Did not fail to object → did object', 'Not every visitor complained → at least one did not', 'Not impossible → possible, not certain']
  SCRIPT: Some negatives cancel neatly, but others do not. ‘She did not fail to object’ says she objected. ‘Not every visitor complained’ says at least one visitor did not complain; it does not say no visitor complained. ‘Not impossible’ means possible, not certain. Work from the inside out and keep the scope of each negative clear. When the wording becomes awkward, replace one small phrase at a time with plain English, then reread the sentence. Do not count negative-looking words and decide that an even number must make the whole sentence positive. Natural language contains different verbs, quantifiers and scopes, so that shortcut can produce the wrong meaning.
[8] Test similar openings efficiently
  BOARD: ['Same opening meaning → reuse the understood clause', 'Different ending → check the new connection', 'Finish the whole sentence before deciding.']
  SCRIPT: If two options have essentially the same opening, you can reuse the understanding you already built and focus on where they differ. This saves effort without skipping a logical check. Once one completion makes the entire sentence coherent and you have a clear reason for rejecting the serious rival, do not keep rereading out of anxiety. In untimed practice, inspect all four so you learn the traps. In timed work, avoid unnecessary checking, but never stop after a promising first clause while later blanks remain untested.
[9] Check the final message
  BOARD: ['Who believes what?', 'Which clause supports or challenges it?', 'Did every blank preserve that connection?']
  SCRIPT: At the end, give a one-sentence summary of the completed sentence. ‘The critic expected a weak performance, but the performance changed her mind.’ ‘The survey supports a limited claim, not a universal one.’ If you cannot state that message, the option may only feel familiar rather than make sense. This final paraphrase is especially useful with reported opinions: the writer may describe a belief without accepting it. Keep the owner of each claim attached to it. Then choose and move on. The practice sets will gradually add longer clauses, counterfactuals and multiple speakers while preserving the same basic routine.

=== VIDEO solve-vr41-g001: kind=solution title='A prediction and its example'
[1] Your question
  BOARD: []
  SCRIPT: The old forecast was described as ____ because its predictions usually matched events; nevertheless, the recent error showed that it was ____. For example, it predicted a rise when sales actually ____.
[2] Keep the skeleton
  BOARD: ['Usually matching events supports reliability.', 'An admitted error rules out infallibility.', 'A fall makes the predicted rise an example of error.']
  SCRIPT: Usually matching events supports reliability. An admitted error rules out infallibility. A fall makes the predicted rise an example of error. Read the sentence with this completion in a natural voice. Pause at each turn and paraphrase the clause you have just completed. The words around a blank tell us what job its phrase has to do. We are checking the links inside this sentence, so outside knowledge about a real forecast, reviewer or repair is unnecessary.
[3] Locate the exact mismatch
  BOARD: ['reliable · not infallible · fell']
  SCRIPT: The fragment “unreliable” does not fit this position. Usually matching events supports reliability. The fragment “infallible” does not fit this position. Notice that a completion can begin well and still fail later. Test the entire thought. Once its three parts work together, say the overall message in one short sentence. That check is more reliable than accepting an option because one familiar phrase sounds right.

  BOARD: ['Choice 4', 'reliable · not infallible · fell']
  SCRIPT: The answer is choice 4: reliable · not infallible · fell. A fall makes the predicted rise an example of error.

# Topic 43 Understanding sentences

######## SECTION vr43-learn — Learn and try (learn)
=== VIDEO vr-sentence-meaning: kind=lesson title='Understanding sentences: scope, emphasis and implication'
[1] Find the main action
  BOARD: ['Who did what?', 'What is denied?', 'What is only being suggested?']
  SCRIPT: A sentence can become difficult without containing a single advanced word. ‘He never missed a chance to avoid the meeting’ is built from ordinary words, but several actions and negatives are nested inside one another. Start with the main subject and action. Then unfold the added phrase. In this case, he took every opportunity to avoid the meeting. That tells us about his repeated attempts or choices, not necessarily whether he managed to avoid every meeting. Plain English is our tool, but the plain version must keep that distinction. When a question asks what can be inferred, we need the meaning supported by the sentence, not an attractive extra story.
[2] Unpack from the inside out
  BOARD: ['Avoid attending → try not to attend', 'Never missed a chance → used every opportunity', 'Not necessarily: succeeded every time']
  SCRIPT: Take the smallest difficult phrase first. Avoid attending means try or arrange not to attend. Now put back ‘never missed a chance.’ He used every opportunity to do that. This inside-out method works better than counting negatives. Some negative-looking verbs express prevention or refusal, and their meanings do not cancel mechanically. If the sentence instead said he failed to avoid attending, he attended despite trying not to. Say the small phrase naturally before rebuilding the full sentence. You should be able to point to which action actually occurred and which one was only attempted, wished for or prevented.
[3] Only changes what is excluded
  BOARD: ['Only Maya praised the design.', 'Maya praised only the design.', 'Maya only praised the design.']
  SCRIPT: These sentences do different jobs. Only Maya praised the design excludes other people from praising it. Maya praised only the design limits the objects of her praise. Maya only praised the design may contrast praising with doing something more, such as funding it, and context can matter. Do not move ‘only’ around while paraphrasing. Attach it to the part it restricts. If a sentence is genuinely ambiguous, a question may ask which interpretation is possible or which revision removes the ambiguity. We should not pretend that English always has just one scope reading. Look at what the wording and surrounding context actually settle.
[4] Separate fact from attitude
  BOARD: ['The proposal was rejected.', 'The speaker regretted the rejection.', 'An event and an opinion about it are different.']
  SCRIPT: ‘Unfortunately, the proposal was rejected’ reports an event and expresses a negative attitude to that event. ‘The proposal was not rejected without reason’ says there was a reason for rejecting it; it does not tell us every reason was good. These small qualifications often decide an answer. Ask two questions: what happened, and what does the speaker think about it? Someone may dislike a decision yet accept that it had a reasonable basis. Someone may support a decision without being pleased about all its consequences. Keep those judgments separate rather than forcing every sentence into a simple approval-or-disapproval label.
[5] Compare exactly what is compared
  BOARD: ['More useful than the old version', 'Does not mean useful in every respect', 'Less expensive does not mean inexpensive']
  SCRIPT: Relative words compare things. A revised manual can be clearer than the old one and still be confusing. A cheaper ticket can still be expensive. When you paraphrase, keep the comparison partner and the direction. If an answer turns ‘more accurate than’ into ‘completely accurate,’ it has made the claim stronger. If it drops ‘for beginners,’ it may have widened the group to which the comparison applies. These are small edits with large logical effects. Your active-reading mark can be a simple greater-than sign, but place the two things and the quality beside it so you remember what is being compared.
[6] Replace a phrase without changing the claim
  BOARD: ['Check subject, time, scope and certainty.', 'A true statement may still change the meaning.']
  SCRIPT: Some questions ask which replacement changes the meaning, or which sentence expresses the same idea. Do not merely ask whether the replacement sounds plausible. Compare it with the original in four respects: who is acting, when the action occurs, how wide the claim is, and how certain it is. ‘Could have arrived’ is not ‘arrived.’ ‘Everyone did not agree’ may be less clear than ‘not everyone agreed.’ ‘The doctor brought the actor to the clinic’ assigns roles differently from ‘the actor brought the doctor.’ Read each complete version once in a natural voice. A role or time change often becomes obvious when you picture the scene.
--- Q vr43-g001: The director criticized the exhibition, and, in the reviewer’s opinion, not without reason. Which statement best represents the reviewer’s view?
  CHOICES: ['The criticism had no basis.', 'The reviewer disliked every part of the exhibition.', 'There was some justification for the criticism.', 'Every criticism made by the director was correct.'] correct=3
=== VIDEO solve-vr43-g001: kind=solution title='The meaning of “not without reason”'
[1] Your question
  BOARD: []
  SCRIPT: The director criticized the exhibition, and, in the reviewer’s opinion, not without reason. Which statement best represents the reviewer’s view?
[2] Put the message in plain English
  BOARD: ['There was some justification for the criticism.']
  SCRIPT: “Not without reason” removes the claim that the criticism was baseless. It supplies some justification, not complete agreement with every detail. Picture the scene with the roles and the time fixed. If you shorten the sentence, leave its logical restriction attached to the same word. That gives us a compact version we can safely compare with the answers.
[3] Keep each answer tied to the text
  BOARD: ['Qualified approval']
  SCRIPT: “Every criticism made by the director was correct.” fails because some justification is weaker than full endorsement. “The criticism had no basis.” fails because this is the meaning explicitly denied. “The reviewer disliked every part of the exhibition.” fails because no judgment about every part of the exhibition is given. We have a reason for rejecting each alternative. That matters more than whether an answer repeats a familiar word from the passage. A strong distractor often borrows the vocabulary while changing the owner, direction or strength of the claim. Return to the exact part of the text that decides between the closest two options.
[4] Choose the matching answer
  BOARD: ['Choice 3', 'There was some justification for the criticism.']
  SCRIPT: The answer is choice 3: There was some justification for the criticism.. “Not without reason” removes the claim that the criticism was baseless. It supplies some justification, not complete agreement with every detail.
